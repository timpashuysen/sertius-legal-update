The action The plea of inadmissibility 22 The Parliament, supported by the Commission, suggests that the Court should hold that the present action has served no purpose since 1 January 2024, the date on which Article 1(5) of Decision 2015/1814 was amended by Article 2 of Directive 2023/959. Substance 27 In support of its action, the Republic of Poland puts forward four pleas in law, alleging an infringement of, first, point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU; second, the principle of energy solidarity referred to in Article 194(1)(b) TFEU; third, Article 3(1) and (3) TEU; and fourth, Article 13(2) TEU, read in conjunction with Article 3 of Decision 2015/1814, and of the principle of proportionality guaranteed in Article 5(4) TEU. First plea in law: infringement of point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU – Arguments of the parties 28 In support of its first plea, the Republic of Poland submits that the contested decision should have been adopted not on the basis of Article 192(1) TFEU but rather point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU, since that decision has a significant impact on the right of at least one Member State to choose from among different energy sources and the general structure of its energy supply. 29 In the first place, the Republic of Poland submits that, when a draft piece of legislation is being drawn up under Article 191 TFEU, the Commission should imperatively examine of its own motion whether the conditions of application of point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU are satisfied. The EU legislature’s decision not to apply that provision should be clear from the travaux préparatoires for the contested decision in order for the Court to be able to review objectively whether the adoption of that decision under Article 192(1) TFEU is contrary to point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU. 30 In the second place, the Republic of Poland submits that the doubling of the quantity of greenhouse gas emission allowances placed in the reserve until 2030 will have the effect of increasing the price of those allowances, which will have a significant impact on the freedom of Member States in defining their energy mix. In that regard, the introduction of a ground for the permanent cancellation of greenhouse gas emission allowances accumulated in that reserve will lead to their permanent elimination from the market, which will lead to a worsening of the deficit of such allowances and, consequently, an increase in their price. Therefore, the growth of the reserve since it was established in 2015 is undeniable, with the result that the considerations set out in the judgment of 21 June 2018, Poland v Parliament and Council (C‑5/16, EU:C:2018:483) concerning the neutrality of the reserve vis-à-vis price levels of the greenhouse gas emission allowances, are no longer relevant. It would, moreover, be contradictory to claim, on the one hand, that the reserve is a mechanism that is neutral as regards developments in the price of greenhouse gas emission allowances and, on the other hand, that, by creating a scarcity of those allowances on the market, it ‘sends a price signal’ intended to influence investors’ operational and strategic decisions. 31 Furthermore, since, by 2030, CO2 capture and storage technology will not be sufficiently developed, it will not be possible to achieve the CO2 emission reduction targets, including the climate neutrality target, without excluding fossil fuels from the Member States’ energy mix. In those circumstances, the neutrality of the EU ETS is illusory. In fact, the ‘Fit for 55’ legislative package, which is a comprehensive set of mutually reinforcing measures and initiatives aimed at achieving climate neutrality in the Union by 2050, seeks by design to reduce significantly the use of coal, which tends to support the choice of point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU as the legal basis for the adoption of the contested decision. Contrary to the Court’s ruling in the judgment of 21 June 2018, Poland v Parliament and Council (C‑5/16, EU:C:2018:483), that provision applies to any measure having a significant effect on a Member State’s energy mix, irrespective of whether the sole or primary purpose of that measure is to have a significant effect on that mix. The criterion of the effects of an act are, moreover, applied in certain areas of EU law, such as competition law or the freedoms of movement. In the present case, the contested decision is liable to deter Member States from using fossil fuels and is thus, by definition, a measure significantly affecting the choice between different energy sources, within the meaning of point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU. 32 On that point, the Republic of Poland submits that that provision constitutes lex specialis . not on the basis of Article 192(1) TFEU but rather point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU, since that decision has a significant impact, at the very least, on its right to choose from among different energy sources and the general structure of its energy supply. 38 In that regard, it should be borne in mind that the choice of point (c) of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU as a legal basis for a measure cannot be founded on factors other than those consistently taken into the account by the Court in its case-law (see, to that effect, judgment of 21 June 2018, Poland v Parliament and Council , C‑5/16, EU:C:2018:483, paragraphs 37 to 39). 39 The Court has consistently held, first, that the choice of the legal basis for a measure may not depend simply on an institution’s conviction as to the objective pursued but must be based on objective factors which are amenable to judicial review, such as the aim and the content of the measure (see, to that effect, judgments of 26 March 1987, Commission v Council , 45/86, EU:C:1987:163, paragraph 11; of 29 April 2004, Commission v Council , C‑338/01, EU:C:2004:253, paragraph 54; and of 16 February 2022, Poland v Parliament and Council , C‑157/21, EU:C:2022:98, paragraph 121). 40 If the choice of legal basis of a legislative measure were to depend on an assessment of the likely impact thereof which, by its nature, is speculative, only an assessment after its entry into force would make it possible to know the real and specific effects of such a measure. That assessment would therefore in no way be an objective factor amenable to judicial review (see, to that effect, judgment of 21 June 2018, Poland v Parliament and Council , C‑5/16, EU:C:2018:483, paragraph 41). 41 It is, moreover, settled case-law that the validity of an EU measure cannot depend on retrospective assessment of its efficacy (judgments of 7 February 1973, Schroeder , 40/72, EU:C:1973:14, paragraph 14; of 5 October 1994, Crispoltoni and Others , C‑133/93, C‑300/93 and C‑362/93, EU:C:1994:364, paragraph 43; and of 12 July 2001, Jippes and Others , C‑189/01, EU:C:2001:420, paragraph 84). 42 Consequently, the assessment of the anticipated effect of an EU measure on a Member State’s energy policy may be taken into consideration only in the assessment of the aim or content of that measure (see, to that effect, judgment of 21 June 2018, Poland v Parliament and Council , C‑5/16, EU:C:2018:483, paragraph 42). 43 Secondly, the choice of legal basis for a measure may also be based on the context of which it forms part, since that context is capable of shedding light on the aim pursued by that measure (see, to that effect, judgments of 27 February 2014, United Kingdom v Council , C‑656/11, EU:C:2014:97, paragraph 50; of 3 December 2019, Czech Republic v Parliament and Council , C‑482/17, EU:C:2019:1035, paragraph 32; of 8 December 2020, Hungary v Parliament and Council , C‑620/18, EU:C:2020:1001, paragraph 39; and of 16 February 2022, Poland v Parliament and Council , C‑157/21, EU:C:2022:98, paragraph 122). 44 On that point, a distinction should be drawn between a measure analogous to a pre-existing measure and a measure that amends a pre-existing measure. According to settled case-law, the legal basis for a measure must be determined having regard to its own aim and content and not to the legal basis used for the adoption of other EU measures that might, in certain cases, display similar characteristics (judgments of 10 January 2006, Commission v Parliament and Council , C‑178/03, EU:C:2006:4, paragraph 55, and of 21 June 2018, Poland v Parliament and Council , C‑5/16, EU:C:2018:483, paragraph 49). However, with respect to legislation which amends existing legislation, it is important also to take into account, for the purposes of identifying its legal basis, the existing legislation that it amends and in particular, its objective and content (judgments of 3 December 2019, Czech Republic v Parliament and Council , C‑482/17, EU:C:2019:1035, paragraph 42, and of 8 December 2020, Hungary v Parliament and Council , C‑620/18, EU:C:2020:1001, paragraph 40). 45 Thirdly, if the examination of an EU measure reveals that it pursues several purposes or that it comprises several components and if one of those purposes or components is identifiable as the main or predominant purpose or component, whereas the others are merely incidental or are only extremely limited in scope, the legal basis for adopting that measure must be determined in accordance with that main purpose or component (see, to that effect, judgments of 23 February 1999, Parliament v Council , C‑42/97, EU:C:1999:81, paragraphs 39 and 40; of 8 December 2020, Hungary v Parliament and Council , C‑620/18, EU:C:2020:1001, paragraph 38; and of 21 March 2024, Landeshauptstadt Wiesbaden , C‑61/22, EU:C:2024:251, paragraph 47). 46 Fourthly, a broad interpretation of the first subparagraph of Article 192(2) TFEU would risk having the effect of making recourse to the special legislative procedure, which the Treaty FEU intended as an exception, into the general rule.
