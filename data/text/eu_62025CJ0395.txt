The dispute in the main proceedings and the questions referred for a preliminary ruling 20 Orkla, a company incorporated under Swedish law, markets chemical products such as liquid laundry detergents. 21 It is apparent from the order for reference and the images set out in the annex thereto that the packaging for those products is made of coloured cardboard, in the shape of an elongated carton, with a folded lid on which a screw cap is fitted on one side of the fold. 22 After being alerted by consumers that that packaging bore similarities to that used for foodstuffs and was likely to mislead consumers, KEMI invited Orkla to submit its comments on that matter and on the compliance of the packaging in question with the requirements of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation. 23 Orkla disputed the existence of any such similarity and argued that the packaging of the products in question did not comply with those requirements. It argued that the packaging in question features depictions of textiles and not of items likely to be mistaken for foodstuffs; that the packaging provides sufficient safety guarantees, given that a seal must be removed before the products can be used and that it bears a warning that the contents must not be ingested; that the products in question are merely irritating to the eyes and pose no danger if ingested; and that the choice of cardboard was motivated by environmental considerations. 24 KEMI considered that the presentation of the packaging of the products at issue in the main proceedings – namely, both its shape and its appearance, in combination with its colouring and the label affixed to it, together with the indication that the product is vegan – was likely to mislead consumers, given that those products could be mistaken for foodstuffs. It therefore considered that that packaging did not meet the requirements of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation and decided to prohibit the placing on the market of nine products, namely liquid laundry detergents contained in that type of packaging. 25 Orkla brought an action before the Mark- och miljödomstolen i Nacka (Land and Environment Court, Nacka, Sweden). That court held that, having regard to the appearance, size and closure mechanism of the packaging of the products at issue in the main proceedings, in conjunction with their colouring and presentation, that packaging could be mistaken for that used to package foodstuffs, in particular milk, yoghurt, fruit juice and other liquid foods, and, as a result, attract and encourage children’s curiosity. Consequently, that court held that KEMI’s decision was in accordance with EU law and, in particular, with the CLP Regulation, and dismissed Orkla’s appeal. It nevertheless amended that decision as regards its date of effect, setting it at three months after its judgment became final. 26 Orkla lodged an appeal against that judgment with the Svea hovrätt, Mark- och miljööverdomstolen (Svea Court of Appeal, Land and Environment Court of Appeal, Stockholm, Sweden), which is the referring court. 27 That court has doubts as to whether the packaging of the products in question, which are substances or mixtures classified as hazardous under the CLP Regulation, complies with the requirements of Article 35(2) of that regulation. 28 In that regard, the referring court asks, in the first place, what criteria should be applied when assessing, in accordance with Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation, the similarity between the presentation or appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture and that used for foodstuffs. That court therefore asks whether the features intended to distinguish such packaging from that of foodstuffs may be removed when the product is first used, or whether they must remain on the packaging intact and permanently. 29 In the second place, it considers the relevant market for the purposes of assessing such similarity under that provision. 30 In the third place, that court is considering how the term ‘consumers’ should be interpreted within the meaning of that provision and, more specifically, whether it is necessary to include vulnerable persons within that category. 31 In the fourth place, it has doubts as to the level of likelihood of confusion on the part of consumers that would be required under that provision, and as to the relevance, in that regard, of the existence of established cases of confusion. 32 In the fifth and last place, the referring court seeks to ascertain whether, for the purposes of assessing whether packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture complies with the requirements laid down in Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation, the category of danger to which the consumer is exposed in the event of confusion and the specific risk associated with exposure to that substance or mixture are decisive. 33 In those circumstances, the Svea hovrätt, Mark- och miljööverdomstolen (Svea Court of Appeal, Land and Environment Court of Appeal, Stockholm), decided to stay proceedings and refer the following questions to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling: ‘(1) On the basis of which detailed criteria must the assessment under Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation be made as to whether packaging has a similar presentation or design to that used, for example, for foodstuffs? Must measures to distinguish packaging from, for example, food packaging, be permanent throughout the life cycle of the packaging or may they be designed to be torn off? (2) Must the assessment of whether packaging has a similar presentation or design to that, for example, for foodstuffs, be made on the basis of the food packaging on the market on which the product is placed or the market on which it is supplied to the public, and must the assessment then be made solely on the basis of the national market, the entire EU market, or parts thereof? (3) How should the term “consumer” be interpreted and applied in Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation? Does it relate to the average consumer or must vulnerable or disadvantaged consumer groups, such as visually impaired persons, persons with other disabilities and children, also be included in the notion of consumer? If so, how must their specific needs be taken into account? (4) What must be the level of likelihood of the consumer being misled into believing that the packaging contains foodstuff? Is it sufficient for there to be a theoretical or insignificant likelihood or must the likelihood be real? Is it relevant to take into account in that assessment whether confusion with foodstuffs has actually occurred, where such information is available? (5) Must the hazard category and the specific risk of the substance or mixture be taken into account when applying Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation? Must that article be interpreted as applying to packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture and having a similar presentation or a design used for foodstuffs, where, as in this case, the contents of the packaging are not hazardous to ingest but are hazardous only in the case of other types of exposure, such as the mixture being capable of causing reversible eye irritation?’ Consideration of the questions referred The first question 34 By its first question, the referring court asks, in essence, first, whether the criteria to be applied in assessing whether packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture supplied to the general public has a presentation or appearance similar to that used for foodstuffs, within the meaning of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation. Secondly, it asks whether that provision must be interpreted as meaning that, for the purposes of that assessment, account must be taken, as a decisive factor, the presence of safety features affixed to packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture, designed to distinguish it from packaging containing foodstuffs, even if those features do not remain intact throughout the entire lifespan of the packaging containing such a substance or mixture. 35 As a preliminary point, it should be noted that, according to Article 3(1) of the CLP Regulation, a substance or mixture which meets the criteria relating to physical hazards, health hazards or environmental hazards, as set out in Parts 2 to 5 of Annex I to that regulation, is hazardous and is classified as such in one of the hazard classes provided for in Annex I. 36 It is undisputed that the packaging of the products at issue in the main proceedings contains a substance or mixture which meets the criteria for a health hazard set out in point 3.3 of Annex I to that regulation, entitled ‘Serious eye damage/eye irritation’, and which is classified in hazard category 2, known as ‘eye irritation’, in accordance with point 3.3.2(b) of that Annex I. 37 Where a substance or mixture is classified as hazardous, Article 4(4) of the CLP Regulation requires suppliers to ensure that that substance or mixture is labelled and packaged in accordance with the provisions of Titles III and IV before it is placed on the market. 38 In that regard, Article 35 of the CLP Regulation, which forms part of Title IV of that regulation, sets out the requirements to be met by packaging containing such substances or mixtures. 39 In particular, the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of that regulation is intended to prevent consumers from using a dangerous product in a manner other than that for which it was designed. Under that provision, packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture supplied to the general public must not have a shape or design likely to attract or encourage children’s active curiosity or to mislead consumers, nor must it have a presentation or design similar to that used for foodstuffs, animal feed, medicinal products or cosmetics, which would mislead consumers. 40 The questions referred by the referring court relate, more specifically, to the second scenario referred to in the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation, namely that the presentation or appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture must not be similar to that used, in particular, for foodstuffs. 41 In the first place, with regard to the criteria on the basis of which it is necessary to determine whether there is a similarity between the presentation and appearance of packaging containing a dangerous substance or mixture and that used for foodstuffs, it should be noted, from the outset, that the wording of Article 35(2) of that regulation does not explicitly refer to such criteria. 42 Nevertheless, the EU legislature, by referring in that wording to broad concepts such as the ‘presentation’ of packaging or its ‘aesthetics’ – separated by the conjunction ‘or’ – intended to take into account all the perceptible characteristics of the packaging of a dangerous substance or mixture. 43 Consequently, in order to assess whether packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture supplied to the general public has a presentation or appearance similar to that used for foodstuffs, which would mislead consumers within the meaning of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation, account must be taken of its overall appearance. 44 In particular, such similarity must be taken into account where, in view of the overall impression created by the packaging containing a dangerous substance or mixture, that packaging resembles that of a foodstuff and is likely to be confused with it on account of objective characteristics such as, in particular, its shape, size, volume, design, colour, labelling, materials of composition, texture, smell or a combination of some or all of those elements, which usually enable the general public to identify foodstuffs. 45 Furthermore, given the wide variety of packaging containing hazardous substances or mixtures, the assessment of whether a given package complies with the requirements of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation must be carried out on a case-by-case basis. 46 In the context of that assessment, account must be taken of the purpose of the CLP Regulation, which, as provided for in Article 1(1) thereof and set out in recital 1 thereof, is aimed, inter alia, at ensuring a high level of protection of human health and the environment. In view of that objective, the packaging requirements arising from the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of that regulation must therefore be assessed in the light of their ability to ensure a high level of protection of consumers’ health. 47 In the second place, as regards the questions referred by the referring court concerning the taking into account, when assessing the similarity between packaging containing a dangerous substance or mixture and that containing a foodstuff, of safety devices affixed to the former packaging, even if those devices are not permanent, in that they do not remain intact throughout the entire lifespan of the packaging containing such a substance or mixture, that court states that, in this case, the issue concerns the presence of a label covering the cap on the packaging, which is intended to be removed upon first use of that substance or mixture. 48 As the wording of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation does not provide any specific guidance in that regard, it is necessary to examine the context in which that provision is set out, as well as the objective pursued by the EU legislature. 49 In that regard, Article 9(5) of that regulation provides that, when assessing the available information for the purposes of classification, manufacturers, importers and downstream users are to take into account the physical forms or states in which the substance or mixture is placed on the market and in which it can reasonably be expected ‘to be used’. 50 Furthermore, Article 35(1)(c) of that regulation stipulates that packaging containing dangerous substances or mixtures must, in particular, meet the requirement that all components of the packaging and closures are strong and robust, so as to prevent any loosening and to withstand safely the stresses and effects normally encountered during handling. 51 Furthermore, with regard to liquid laundry detergents intended for consumers and packaged in soluble, single-use packaging, it follows from point 3.3.2 of Annex II to the CLP Regulation that such packaging must retain its functionality under conditions of repeated opening and closing for ‘the entire life span of the outer packaging’. 52 It follows from those provisions that a safety device which covers and seals the cap fitted to a container holding a dangerous product must remain in place for the entire lifespan of that container. In the present case, as the products in question are liquid laundry detergents, for which the safety device consists of a label, it should be noted that, as that type of product is used regularly and is therefore present in households over the long term, the purpose of that label does not cease to exist after the product has been used for the first time. 53 That interpretation is supported by the objective of ensuring a high level of protection of human health, which is pursued by the CLP Regulation. As the Advocate General noted, in essence, in paragraph 41 of his Opinion, the CLP Regulation aims to protect not only the purchaser of a hazardous substance or mixture at the time of the purchase decision, but also any potential user of such a substance or mixture, and that applies throughout the lifespan of the product in question, since the risk of confusion between that product and foodstuffs may exist both at the time of purchase and during the subsequent use of that product. 54 As regards the presence of a warning on the packaging stating, in this case, that the hazardous substance or mixture contained therein must not be ingested – to which reference is also made in the documents submitted to the Court – it should be noted that, as is apparent from Articles 17 to 22 of the CLP Regulation, packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture must necessarily bear such information and be designed in such a way that it cannot be damaged or that its contents cannot be lost. Those requirements apply in addition to those set out in the second part of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of that regulation. Furthermore, statements and warnings indicating that the hazardous product in question must not be ingested do not necessarily counteract the immediate visual impact on consumers of the presentation or appearance of the packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture which, given its similarity to that used for foodstuffs, is likely to be mistaken for packaging containing such foodstuffs. 55 In this case, such an additional safety feature, just like the presence of a label covering the cap on the packaging – which seals the opening and must be torn off when the substance or mixture is used for the first time – in so far as they do not necessarily alter the overall impression created by that packaging, cannot, in themselves, be sufficient to rule out the possibility that the consumer might be misled into confusing that packaging with, in particular, that of foodstuffs, in breach of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation. 56 In view of the foregoing, the answer to the first question is that the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation must be interpreted as meaning that the assessment of whether packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture supplied to the general public has a presentation or appearance similar to that used, in particular, for foodstuffs, which would mislead consumers, within the meaning of that provision: – must be carried out on a case-by-case basis, taking into account the overall appearance of the packaging and, in particular, its objective characteristics, such as, amongst others, its shape, size, volume, design, colour, labelling, materials used, texture, smell or a combination of some or all of those elements; – does not imply that the presence of safety features affixed to packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture, intended to distinguish it from packaging containing foodstuffs, is to be taken into account as a determining factor, where such features do not remain intact throughout the entire lifespan of the packaging containing such a substance or mixture. The second question 57 By its second question, the referring court asks, in essence, whether the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CPL Regulation must be interpreted as meaning that, for the purposes of assessing the similarity between the presentation or appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture supplied to the general public and that used for foodstuffs, account should be taken solely of the market in the Member State concerned or of the markets in other Member States where that substance or mixture is marketed. 58 That court states that KEMI is competent to carry out market surveillance only in respect of product packaging placed on the Swedish market, and expresses doubts as to the meaning of the phrase ‘supplied to the general public’ in that provision. 59 In accordance with settled case-law, in order to interpret a provision of EU law, it is necessary to consider not only its wording but also the context in which it occurs and the objectives pursued by the rules of which it is part (see, to that effect, judgment of 17 April 2018, Egenberger , C‑414/16, EU:C:2018:257, paragraph 44 and the case-law cited). 60 First of all, as regards the wording of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CPL Regulation, it follows that the packaging containing a dangerous substance or mixture to which that provision refers is that supplied to the ‘general public’. That simple statement, without further clarification, does not in itself make it possible to determine what the relevant market is for the purposes of assessing compliance with the requirements laid down in that provision. 61 As regards, next, the context in which the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of that regulation is set, it should be noted that, as is apparent from Article 43 of that regulation, it entrusts the enforcement and supervision of the rules laid down therein to the Member States through their competent authorities, which are responsible, in particular, for implementing the obligations set out in that regulation. Those national authorities are responsible for ensuring the effectiveness of market surveillance, by ensuring that products comply with the applicable Union harmonisation legislation and by safeguarding, within their territory, the public interest covered by that legislation. 62 Under Article 46(1) of the CLP Regulation, Member States must take all necessary measures, including the implementation of a system of official controls, to ensure that substances and mixtures are not placed on the market unless they have been classified, labelled, notified and packaged in accordance with the provisions of that regulation. Paragraph 1a of Article 46 refers, for the purposes of paragraph 1 thereof, to Regulation 2019/1020, which applies, in accordance with Article 2 of, and point 25 of Annex I to, that regulation, to products falling within the scope of the CLP Regulation. 63 It is important to note that Article 3(4) of Regulation 2019/1020 defines a market surveillance authority as an authority designated by a Member State responsible for carrying out market surveillance ‘in the territory of that Member State’. Furthermore, under Article 11(1)(a) of Regulation 2019/1020, market surveillance authorities carry out their activities with a view to ensuring the effective market surveillance of products made available both online and offline ‘within their territory’ in respect of products subject to Union harmonisation legislation. 64 Accordingly, having regard to the provisions referred to in paragraphs 61 to 63 of this judgment, it must be held that, when assessing whether packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture complies with the requirements of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation, the supervisory authority of a Member State must take into account the fact that such packaging resembles a product that is well-known and recognised in that State. Moreover, the specific assessment by the competent national authorities of the risk of misleading consumers, within the meaning of that provision, necessarily involves taking into account the perception of the packaging by those consumers. That assessment must be carried out in relation to the public actually exposed to the product at issue on the national market where it is made available to that public. 65 Such an interpretation is, moreover, supported by Article 11(9) of Regulation 2019/1020, from which it follows that, without prejudice to the Union’s safeguard procedures provided for by the applicable Union harmonisation legislation, the presumption of non-conformity of products arising from a decision to that effect by a market surveillance authority of a Member State – which is binding on the authorities of the other Member States – may be rebutted by a decision of one of those authorities reaching a contrary conclusion following an investigation conducted by it. 66 That power granted to national authorities to reach a different conclusion as to the non-conformity of a given product, on the basis of a possible difference in assessment, is based on the EU legislature’s recognition that, even in the context of full harmonisation of labelling and packaging rules, the perception of that product and the assessment of a likelihood of confusion in the minds of the public may not be the same throughout the Union. Such a power is based on the need to take into account factors such as, in this case, the customary presentation of certain products, specific to a given national market, as well as the perception of those products by consumers in that market. 67 It should also be noted that an interpretation of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation to the effect that that provision implies that national authorities assessing the similarity between products may not take the national market as their reference market, but are obliged to take into account that of other Member States, or even of all Member States, would be difficult to reconcile with one of the objectives of that regulation, which is to ensure a high level of protection of human health. The mere finding that there is a similarity between the presentation or appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture and that used for foodstuffs in a Member State should be sufficient to enable the competent national authorities to take the necessary decisions to protect human health, as the risk of confusion must be assessed in relation to the public actually exposed to the product in question. 68 Finally, the interpretation of the first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of the CLP Regulation, as set out in paragraph 64 of this judgment, is not contrary to the objective pursued by that regulation of ensuring a uniform framework governing the requirements applicable to substances and mixtures with a view to ensuring, in particular, their free movement within the Union, as set out in Article 1(1)(a) of that regulation, read in the light of recital 2 of that regulation. 69 That objective is achieved by a uniform determination of the criteria for assessing the similarity between the presentation or appearance of packaging containing dangerous substances or mixtures and that used for foodstuffs throughout the territory of the Union and, as the Advocate General also observed, in essence, in point 52 of his Opinion, is not called into que

The first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of Regulation No 1272/2008, as amended by Regulation No 1297/2014 and by Regulation 2024/2865, must be interpreted as meaning that, for the purposes of assessing the similarity between the presentation or appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture and that used for foodstuffs, in accordance with the harmonised rules on the labelling and packaging of hazardous substances and mixtures, the competent authorities of a Member State may take into account factors specific to the national market on which the product in question is placed on the market, without it being necessary to examine whether that presentation or appearance would also be regarded as misleading in other Member States within whose territory that product is placed on the market. 3. The first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of Regulation No 1272/2008, as amended by Regulation No 1297/2014 and by Regulation 2024/2865, must be interpreted as meaning that the term ‘consumers’, within the meaning of that provision, refers both to the average consumer who is reasonably well-informed and reasonably observant and circumspect, and to children and vulnerable persons who may have particular difficulty in perceiving or assessing the appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture. When assessing the similarity between the presentation or appearance of packaging containing a hazardous substance or mixture and that used for foodstuffs, within the meaning of that provision – by means of an assessment of the general appearance of that packaging – account must be taken of the ability of those persons to perceive the characteristics of that packaging. 4. The first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of Regulation No 1272/2008, as amended by Regulation No 1297/2014 and Regulation 2024/2865, must be interpreted as meaning that the fact that the risk of misleading consumers is characterised as non-negligible is sufficient to conclude that the requirements laid down in that provision are not met, without proof of a previous instance of actual confusion between a given package containing a hazardous substance or mixture and one containing a foodstuff being decisive in that regard. 5. The first subparagraph of Article 35(2) of Regulation No 1272/2008, as amended by Regulation No 1297/2014 and Regulation 2024/2865, must be interpreted as meaning that, for the purposes of assessing whether the packaging of a hazardous substance or mixture complies with the requirements laid down in that provision, neither the hazard class or category to which that substance or mixture belongs nor the specific risk associated with it is decisive.
