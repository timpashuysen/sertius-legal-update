I. Legal context A. The REACH Regulation 2 Article 59 of Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 18 December 2006 concerning the Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH), establishing a European Chemicals Agency, amending Directive 1999/45/EC and repealing Council Regulation (EEC) No 793/93 and Commission Regulation (EC) No 1488/94 as well as Council Directive 76/769/EEC and Commission Directives 91/155/EEC, 93/67/EEC, 93/105/EC and 2000/21/EC ( OJ 2006 L 396, p. 1 , and corrigendum OJ 2007 L 136, p. 3 ; ‘the REACH Regulation’), provides, in paragraph 4 thereof: ‘The [European Chemicals Agency (ECHA)] shall publish on its website a notice that an Annex XV dossier has been prepared for a substance. [ECHA] shall invite all interested parties to submit comments within a specified deadline to [ECHA].’ 3 Article 64(1) to (5) of the REACH Regulation provides: ‘1 [ECHA] shall acknowledge the date of receipt of the application. [ECHA’s] Committees for Risk Assessment [(“the RAC”)] and Socio-economic Analysis shall give their draft opinions within ten months of the date of receipt of the application. 2. [ECHA] shall make available on its web-site broad information on uses, taking into account Articles 118 and 119 on access to information, for which applications have been received and for reviews of authorisations, with a deadline by which information on alternative substances or technologies may be submitted by interested third parties. 3. In preparing its opinion, each Committee referred to in paragraph 1 shall first check that the application includes all the information specified in Article 62 that is relevant to its remit. If necessary, the Committees shall, in consultation with each other, make a joint request to the applicant for additional information to bring the application into conformity with the requirements of Article 62. The Committee for Socio-economic Analysis may, if it deems it necessary, require the applicant or request third parties to submit, within a specified time period, additional information on possible alternative substances or technologies. Each Committee shall also take into account any information submitted by third parties. 4. The draft opinions shall include the following elements: (a) [RAC]: an assessment of the risk to human health and/or the environment arising from the use(s) of the substance, including the appropriateness and effectiveness of the risk management measures as described in the application and, if relevant, an assessment of the risks arising from possible alternatives; (b) Committee for Socio-economic Analysis: an assessment of the socio-economic factors and the availability, suitability and technical feasibility of alternatives associated with the use(s) of the substance as described in the application, when an application is made in accordance with Article 62 and of any third party contributions submitted under paragraph 2 of this Article. 5. [ECHA] shall send these draft opinions to the applicant by the end of the deadline set out in paragraph 1. Within one month of receipt of the draft opinion, the applicant may provide written notice that he wishes to comment. The draft opinion shall be deemed to have been received seven days after [ECHA] has sent it. If the applicant does not wish to comment, [ECHA] shall send these opinions to the [European] Commission, the Member States and the applicant, within 15 days of the end of the period within which the applicant may comment or within 15 days of receipt of notice from the applicant that he does not intend to comment. If the applicant wishes to comment, he shall send his written argumentation to [ECHA] within two months of the receipt of the draft opinion. The Committees shall consider the comments and adopt their final opinions within two months of receipt of the written argumentation, taking this argumentation into account where appropriate. Within a further 15 days [ECHA] shall send the opinions, with the written argumentation attached, to the Commission, the Member States and the applicant.’ B. The CLP Regulation 4 Recital 52 of Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 16 December 2008 on classification, labelling and packaging of substances and mixtures, amending and repealing Directives 67/548/EEC and 1999/45/EC, and amending Regulation (EC) No 1907/2006 ( OJ 2008 L 353, p. 1 ), as amended by Commission Regulation (EU) 2019/521 of 27 March 2019 ( OJ 2019 L 86, p. 1 ) (‘the CLP Regulation’), states: ‘The resources of the authorities should be focused on substances of the highest concern with regard to health and to the environment. Provision should therefore be made to enable competent authorities and manufacturers, importers and downstream users to submit proposals to [ECHA] for a harmonised classification and labelling of substances classified for carcinogenicity, germ cell mutagenicity or reproductive toxicity categories 1A, 1B or 2, for respiratory sensitisation, or in respect of other effects on a case-by-case basis. The competent authorities of Member States should also be able to propose harmonised classification and labelling for active substances used in plant protection products and biocidal products. [ECHA] should give its opinion on the proposal while interested parties should have an opportunity to comment. The Commission should submit a draft decision on the final classification and labelling elements.’ 5 Article 1(1)(d) of the CLP Regulation provides: ‘The purpose of this Regulation is to ensure a high level of protection of human health and the environment as well as the free movement of substances, mixtures and articles as referred to in Article 4(8) by: … (d) establishing a list of substances with their harmonised classifications and labelling elements at Community level in Part 3 of Annex VI’. 6 The first paragraph of Article 3 of that regulation provides: ‘A substance or a mixture fulfilling the criteria relating to physical hazards, health hazards or environmental hazards, laid down in Parts 2 to 5 of Annex I is hazardous and shall be classified in relation to the respective hazard classes provided for in that Annex.’ 7 Article 37(1), (2), (4) and (5) of that regulation provides: ‘1. A competent authority may submit to [ECHA] a proposal for harmonised classification and labelling of substances and, where appropriate, specific concentration limits or [multiplying factors; “M-factors”], or a proposal for a revision thereof. The proposal shall follow the format set out in Part 2 of Annex VI and contain the relevant information provided for in Part 1 of Annex VI. 2. A manufacturer, importer or downstream user of a substance may submit to [ECHA] a proposal for harmonised classification and labelling of that substance and, where appropriate, specific concentration limits or M-factors, provided that there is no entry in Part 3 of Annex VI for such a substance in relation to the hazard class or differentiation covered by that proposal. The proposal shall be drawn up in accordance with the relevant Parts of sections 1, 2 and 3 of Annex I to [the REACH Regulation] and it shall follow the format set out in Part B of the Chemical Safety Report of section 7 of that Annex. It shall contain the relevant information provided for in Part 1 of Annex VI to this Regulation. Article 111 of [the REACH Regulation] shall apply. … 4. The [RAC] set up pursuant to Article 76(1)(c) of [the REACH Regulation] shall adopt an opinion on any proposal submitted pursuant to paragraphs 1 or 2 within 18 months of receipt of the proposal, giving the parties concerned the opportunity to comment. [ECHA] shall forward this opinion and any comments to the Commission. 5. The Commission shall without undue delay adopt delegated acts in accordance with Article 53a, where it finds that the harmonisation of the classification and labelling of the substance concerned is appropriate, to amend Annex VI by inclusion of that substance together with the relevant classification and labelling elements in Table 3.1 of Part 3 of Annex VI and, where appropriate, the specific concentration limits or M-factors. …’ 8 Article 50(1) of that regulation provides: ‘[ECHA] shall provide the Member States and the institutions of the [European] Community with the best possible scientific and technical advice on questions relating to chemicals which fall within its remit and which are referred to it in accordance with this Regulation.’ 9 Section 1.1.1.3 of Annex I to the CLP Regulation provides: ‘A weight of evidence determination means that all available information bearing on the determination of hazard is considered together, such as … relevant … data [from] information from the application of the category approach (grouping, read-across), (Q)SAR results …’ 10 Section 3.6.2.2.1 of that annex provides as follows: ‘Classification as a carcinogen is made on the basis of evidence from reliable and acceptable studies and is intended to be used for substances which have an intrinsic property to cause cancer. The evaluations shall be based on all existing data, peer-reviewed published studies and additional acceptable data.’ 11 Section 3.9.1.1 of that annex provides: ‘Specific target organ toxicity-repeated exposure means specific toxic effects on target organs occurring after repeated exposure to a [substance] or mixture. All significant health effects that can impair function, reversible and irreversible, immediate and/or delayed are included. However, other specific toxic effects that are specifically addressed in sections 3.1 to 3.8 and 3.10 are not included here.’ 12 Section 3.9.2.1 of that annex provides: ‘Substances are classified as specific target organ toxicants following repeated exposure by the use of expert judgement (see 1.1.1) on the basis of the weight of all evidence available, including the use of recommended guidance values which take into account the duration of exposure and the dose/concentration which produced the effect(s), (see 3.9.2.9), and are placed in one of two categories, depending upon the nature and severity of the effect(s) observed (Table 3.9.1).’ 13 Section 3.9.2.3 of Annex I to the CLP Regulation provides: ‘Classification is determined by expert judgement (see section 1.1.1), on the basis of the weight of all evidence available including the guidance presented below.’ 14 Section 3.9.2.4 of that annex provides: ‘Weight of evidence of all data (see section 1.1.1), including human incidents, epidemiology, and studies conducted in experimental animals, is used to substantiate specific target organ toxic effects that merit classification. This taps the considerable body of industrial toxicology data collected over the years. Evaluation shall be based on all existing data, including peer-reviewed published studies and additional acceptable data.’ 15 Under Section 3.9.2.10.3 of that annex: ‘A substance that has not been tested for specific target organ toxicity may, where appropriate, be classified on the basis of data from a validated structure activity relationship and expert judgement-based extrapolation from a structural analogue that has previously been classified together with substantial support from consideration of other important factors such as formation of common significant metabolites.’ II. Background to the proceedings and the regulation at issue 16 The background to the dispute, as set out in paragraphs 2 to 10 of the judgment under appeal, may be summarised as follows. 17 The appellant is a company incorporated under German law which manufactures silanamine, 1,1,1-trimethyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)-, hydrolysis products with silica; pyrogenic, synthetic amorphous, nano, surface treated silicon dioxide (SAS-HMDS) (‘silanamine’). 18 It is a type of synthetic amorphous silica (‘SAS’), with a molecular formula of ‘[SiO2]n-[OSi(CH3)3]m’ and which has been treated with hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) to become hydrophobic. SAS, including surface treated forms, are used in various products, such as medicinal and pharmaceutical products, food and cosmetics, and also in a variety of industrial applications, as reinforcement and thickening agents in various systems such as elastomers, resins and inks. 19 Silanamine is also an active substance within the meaning of Regulation (EU) No 528/2012 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 22 May 2012 concerning the making available on the market and use of biocidal products ( OJ 2012 L 167, p. 1 ). 20 On 17 December 2018, the Agence nationale de sécurité sanitaire de l’alimentation, de l’environnement et du travail (National Agency for Food, Environmental and Occupational Health and Safety (ANSES) (France) submitted to ECHA, in accordance with Article 37(1) of the CLP Regulation, a proposal for the harmonised classification and labelling of silanamine (‘the classification and labelling proposal’) in the hazard class ‘Specific target organ toxicity – repeated exposure’ (‘the hazard class STOT RE’) of Category 2 (‘the hazard class STOT RE 2’). 21 Between 4 March 2019 and 3 May 2019, several of the parties concerned submitted their comments on the classification and labelling proposal, in accordance with Article 37(4) of the CLP Regulation. 22 On the basis of that provision, the RAC adopted an opinion dated 5 December 2019 (‘the RAC Opinion’), in which it proposed the classification of silanamine in the hazard class STOT RE 2, with hazard statement code ‘H373’ (lungs, inhalation), as well as in the Category 2 acute-toxicity hazard class via the inhalation route (H330) with an acute toxicity estimate of 0.45 milligram per litre (mg/L). 23 During the period from 3 February to 17 February 2020, a targeted public consultation was organised on the classification of silanamine in the Category 2 acute-toxicity hazard class via the inhalation route (H330). 24 On 16 February 2022, on the basis of the RAC Opinion, the Commission adopted the regulation at issue. By that regulation, the Commission added silanamine to Part 3 of Table 3 of Annex VI to the CLP Regulation, with harmonised classification and labelling in the hazard class STOT RE 2, hazard statement code ‘H373’ (lungs, inhalation) and pictogram hazard code ‘GHS 08Wng’. 25 By contrast, the Commission considered that the classification of silanamine in the Category 2 acute-toxicity hazard class, recommended in the RAC Opinion, should not be included in that part 3, since that institution took the view, after assessing the new scientific information received, that that information required further assessment by the RAC. III. The action before the General Court and the judgment under appeal 26 By application lodged at the Registry of the General Court on 20 July 2022, the appellant brought an action for annulment of the regulation at issue. 27 In support of that action, the appellant raised four pleas in law. The first plea alleged manifest errors of assessment and breach of the criteria for classification of a substance in the hazard class STOT RE Category 2. By the second plea, the appellant alleged a breach of the harmonised classification and labelling procedure (‘the CLH procedure’). The third plea alleged breach of the Commission’s obligation to examine the appropriateness of the contested classification. By the fourth plea, the appellant alleged an absence of any impact assessment. 28 By the judgment under appeal, the General Court dismissed that action and ordered the appellant to pay the costs. IV. Forms of order sought by the parties 29 By its appeal, the appellant claims that the Court should: – declare the appeal admissible and well-founded; – set aside the judgment under appeal; – alternatively, refer the case back to the General Court to rule on the action for annulment, and – order the Commission to pay the costs of the appeal proceedings and the costs incurred in the proceedings at first instance. 30 The Commission contends that the Court should: – dismiss the appeal and – order the appellant to pay the costs. 31 ECHA contends that the Court should: – dismiss the appeal and – order the appellant to pay the costs. V. The appeal 32 In support of its appeal, the appellant puts forward two grounds of appeal. The first ground of appeal alleges that the General Court erred in its examination of the lawfulness of the regulation at issue in the light of the harmonised classification and labelling criteria laid down in the CLP Regulation. The second ground of appeal alleges errors made by the General Court in its interpretation of Article 37(4) of that regulation. A. The first ground of appeal 33 The first ground of appeal is divided into four parts. The first part of that ground of appeal alleges breach of the general principle, to which the General Court referred in the judgment of 23 November 2022, CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission (T‑279/20, T‑283/20 and T‑288/20, ‘the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission ’, EU:T:2022:725 , paragraph 136 ), according to which any hazard supporting a classification, within the meaning of the CLP Regulation, must be the consequence of the intrinsic properties of the substance under review. The second part of that ground of appeal alleges misrepresentation of the argument put forward at first instance by the appellant that the RAC had failed to consider the conditions of testing when drawing conclusions on the STOT RE properties of silanamine and, therefore, a lack of judicial review in that regard. The third part of that ground of appeal alleges that the General Court failed to examine whether the contested classification was based on significant health effects. The fourth part of that ground of appeal alleges errors made by the General Court in its examination of the merits of the reliance, in the RAC Opinion, on a read-across with an SAS that is sufficiently similar to silanamine (‘the read-across’). 1. The first part of the first ground of appeal 34 The first part of the first ground of appeal, alleging breach of the general principle that any hazard supporting classification, within the meaning of the CLP Regulation, must be the consequence of the intrinsic properties of the substance under review, is divided into three subsections. The first and second subsections of that part, which it is appropriate to examine together, allege errors made by the General Court in the application of the requirements arising from that general principle. The third subsection of that part alleges that the General Court failed to review whether the intrinsic properties of silanamine supported the contested classification. (a) The first and second subsections of the first part of the first ground of appeal (1) Arguments of the parties 35 In the context of the first and second subsections of the first part of the first ground of appeal, alleging errors made by the General Court in the application of the requirements arising from the general principle that any hazard supporting classification, within the meaning of the CLP Regulation, must be the consequence of the intrinsic properties of the substance under review, the appellant raises, in essence, three complaints. 36 By the first complaint of those first and second subsections, the appellant complains that, in paragraphs 105, 106 and 113 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court incorrectly distinguished the concepts of ‘specific toxic effects’ and ‘intrinsic properties’, within the meaning of the CLP Regulation, which led it wrongly and contrary to paragraphs 119 and 146 of that judgment to reach a conclusion opposite to that which it had reached in the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission . It submits that, in order to support a classification under that regulation, including in the hazard class STOT RE 2, the observed toxic effects produced by a substance must, as the General Court found in paragraph 139 of that latter judgment, be specific to that substance in that they should be the result of a hazardous, intrinsic property of that substance, and not, as the General Court wrongly understood from its arguments at first instance, in that they must be specific to the same substance. 37 By the second complaint of the first and second subsections, the appellant complains, in essence, that the General Court referred only partially to the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission and thus failed to find, despite other contradictory passages of the judgment under appeal, that the aim of harmonised classification and labelling is, inter alia, to determine the intrinsic properties of substances. 38 By the third complaint of the first and second subsections of the first part of the first ground of appeal, the appellant complains, in essence, that the General Court wrongly distinguished, in paragraphs 146 to 150 of the judgment under appeal, the case before it from that which gave rise to the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission . First, the fact that the classification criteria and the substances concerned, the reasoning set out in the RAC opinions issued and the scientific studies taken into consideration by the RAC were different in those two cases is irrelevant since, according to that latter judgment, all harmonised classifications under the CLP Regulation are based on the concepts of ‘intrinsic properties’ and ‘specific effects’, which are causally linked. Second, contrary to what it did in paragraph 147 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court should not rely on RAC opinions to determine whether effects observed result from the intrinsic properties of the substance concerned and, in any event, the RAC expressed doubts that that was not the case here. Third, the General Court failed to take account of the fact that the toxicity observed in the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission was not specific to the substance at issue, but was common to other poorly soluble low-toxicity substances, and that both that substance and, by analogy, silanamine had unspecific particle-related inhalation toxicity. 39 The Commission and ECHA submit that the first and second subsections of the first part of the first ground of appeal must be rejected as unfounded. (2) Findings of the Court 40 As a preliminary point, it should be noted that the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission was the subject of an appeal before the Court of Justice. In the judgment of 1 August 2025, France and Commission v CWS Powder Coatings and Others ( C‑71/23 P and C‑82/23 P , EU:C:2025:601 ), the Court of Justice did not endorse the General Court’s interpretation in that first judgment as regards the concept of ‘intrinsic properties’ and that of ‘[substance] which [has] an intrinsic property to cause cancer’, within the meaning of Section 3.6.2.2.1 of Annex I to the CLP Regulation. 41 In paragraphs 96 to 173 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court examined and rejected the first part of the first plea raised at first instance, by which the appellant claimed that, for the purposes of classifying a substance in the hazard class STOT RE, that substance must produce specific toxic effects, namely effects which result from its intrinsic properties, and that silanamine did not produce such effects, the effects observed having resulted from the deposition of large numbers of inhaled particles in the context of the relevant studies and from the high dose or concentration administered in those studies. 42 It is apparent from paragraph 121 of the judgment under appeal that it was in the light of the considerations set out in paragraphs 96 to 120 of that judgment that the General Court examined the arguments put forward by the appellant in support of the first part of the first plea raised at first instance. 43 The General Court found, inter alia, in paragraphs 104 to 106 of the judgment under appeal, that the term ‘specific’ in the concept of ‘specific toxic effects’, provided for in Section 3.9.1.1 of Annex I to the CLP Regulation, refers to the target organ, without it being required that those toxic effects be specific to the substance concerned. In paragraph 113 of that judgment, the General Court pointed out that, in the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission , it had interpreted the concept of ‘intrinsic properties’, which relates to the cause of the hazard, without ruling on the concept of ‘specific toxic effects’, which refers to the result of the hazard. 44 In addition, in paragraphs 117 to 120 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court pointed out, referring to paragraphs 136 and 138 to 141 of the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission , that it followed therefrom that, first, the CLP Regulation concerns the transmission of information on the hazards linked to the substances’ intrinsic properties, second, the concept of ‘intrinsic properties’ must be interpreted as referring to the properties which a substance ‘has in and of itself’ and, third, such an interpretation was consistent, inter alia, with the fact that classification and labelling, within the meaning of that regulation, relate to hazard assessment, and not risk assessment, since hazard assessment must not be limited to taking into consideration specific circumstances of use and may be properly carried out regardless of the place of use of the substance concerned or the possible levels of exposure to it. 45 The General Court then examined, in paragraphs 122 to 151 of the judgment under appeal, the appellant’s arguments alleging that the effects observed resulted from the deposition of a large number of inhaled particles in the context of the relevant studies before assessing, in paragraphs 152 to 173 of that judgment, its arguments alleging that the effects observed resulted from the high dose or concentration administered in the relevant studies. 46 In the context of the assessment of that first group of arguments, the General Court noted, in the first place, in paragraph 128 of the judgment under appeal, that the appellant had merely reiterated the argument of the industry put forward during the procedure for the adoption of the regulation at issue that the effects observed in the studies taken into consideration in the RAC Opinion resulted from the deposition of particles inhaled into the lungs which caused an inflammatory response, without showing that that the morphological tissue reaction, consisting of several effects observed, could not form the basis of the contested classification in that those effects were also inflammatory effects resulting from the deposition of a large number of inhaled particles in the studies taken into account. In paragraph 129 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court recalled that, as regards complex facts, it was not its role to substitute its assessment for that made by the RAC, as accepted by the Commission. 47 In the second place, in paragraphs 130 to 144 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court found that the RAC and the Commission had taken into account the industry’s arguments relied on in the course of the procedure for the adoption of the RAC Opinion, but had ultimately rejected them. 48 In the third place, the General Court found, in paragraph 145 of the judgment under appeal, that the analogy drawn by the appellant between the case before it and that which gave rise to the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission was unfounded. 49 In that regard, it is apparent from paragraphs 146 to 148 of the judgment under appeal that the General Court considered that the classification criteria, the substance concerned, the reasoning set out in the RAC Opinion and the scientific studies taken into consideration by that committee in the case which gave rise to the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission were different from those at issue in the present case. 50 In addition, the General Court noted, in paragraph 149 of the judgment under appeal, that it had indeed found, in the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission , that an accumulation of particles in the lungs in sufficient quantities to bring about a significant impairment of particle clearance mechanisms, which occurred only when certain quantities of particles had been inhaled, could not be regarded as forming part of the intrinsic properties of the particles at issue. In paragraph 150 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court pointed out that the specific circumstances referred to in paragraph 158 of the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission did not, however, exist in the present case. 51 In paragraph 150 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court pointed out that, in the regulation at issue, the classification at issue was not linked to a specific size of the particles at issue, unlike that of titanium dioxide in the case which gave rise to the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission . It added, in paragraph 150, that there was no assessment or quantified data in the file to suggest that the effects identified had been observed in target organ overload conditions, as had been the situation in that case. 52 In that regard, it should be noted, as a preliminary point, that a substance or mixture fulfilling the criteria relating to physical hazards, health hazards or environmental hazards, laid down in Parts 2 to 5 of Annex I to the CLP Regulation is, under the first paragraph of Article 3 thereof, considered to be hazardous and is to be classified in relation to the respective hazard classes provided for in that annex. 53 Section 3.9 of Annex I to the CLP Regulation, entitled ‘Specific target organ toxicity – repeated exposure’, which appears in Part 3 thereof, entitled ‘Health hazards’, lays down (i) criteria for classification in the hazard class STOT RE and (ii) requirements relating to the communication of that hazard. 54 Section 3.9.1.1 of that annex defines ‘Specific target organ toxicity-repeated exposure’ as ‘specific toxic effects on target organs occurring after repeated exposure to a [substance] or mixture. All significant health effects that can impair function, reversible and irreversible, immediate and/or delayed are included. However, other specific toxic effects that are specifically addressed in sections 3.1 to 3.8 and 3.10 are not included here’. 55 As regards the first complaint raised in the first and second subsections of the first part of the first ground of appeal, alleging that the distinction between the concepts of ‘specific toxic effects’ and ‘intrinsic properties’ is incorrect, it must admittedly be noted that, in paragraphs 104 to 106 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court drew a distinction between the concepts of ‘specific toxic effects’ and ‘intrinsic properties’ referred to in the CLP Regulation in order to explain that the term ‘specific’ included in the concept of ‘specific toxic effects’, which appears in Section 3.9.1.1 of Annex I to that regulation, refers to the target organ rather than the substance concerned. 56 However, in paragraphs 128 to 150 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court did not rely on the finding that it is not required that the effects observed in the context of the relevant studies in the present case be specific to the substance concerned in order to reject as unfounded the appellant’s argument that those effects resulted not from the intrinsic properties of silanamine, but from the deposition of a large number of inhaled particles in the context of the relevant studies. 57 It is apparent from paragraph 128 of the judgment under appeal that the General Court rejected that argument in so far as, inter alia, it was based on an unsubstantiated claim and, as is apparent from paragraph 150 of that judgment, it refused to reach a conclusion comparable to that which it had reached in the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission , since, in particular, the regulation at issue did not state that the contested classification was linked to a specific size of the particles at issue and since no assessment or numerical data in the file supports the conclusion that the effects identified were observed in lung overload conditions. 58 Similarly, in paragraphs 152 to 172 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court rejected the appellant’s argument that the effects observed result from the high dose or concentration administered in the relevant studies without relying on the finding referred to in paragraph 56 of the present judgment. 59 Accordingly, even assuming that, in paragraphs 104 to 106 of the judgment under appeal, the General Court erred in distinguishing between the concepts of ‘specific toxic effects’ and ‘intrinsic properties’, within the meaning of the CLP Regulation, that error did not affect the merits of its decision to reject the argument alleging that silanamine produces no specific toxic effects on the lungs. 60 Furthermore, since the General Court examined the merits of the appellant’s argument that the toxic effects observed in the context of the relevant studies did not result from the intrinsic properties of silanamine, the alleged misrepresentation of that argument in paragraph 105 of the judgment under appeal, as summarised in paragraph 36 of the present judgment, must be rejected as ineffective. 61 Accordingly, the first complaint raised in the first and second subsections of the first part of the first ground of appeal must be rejected as ineffective. 62 As regards the second complaint of those subsections, by which the appellant submits that the General Court should have held, as the appellant claims that it did in paragraphs 135 and 138 to 140 of the judgment in CWS Powder Coatings and Others v Commission , appeal, the General Court specifically adopted such an interpretation of that regulation and that (ii) it examined the merits of the appellant’s argument that the effects observed in the context of the relevant studies did not result from the intrinsic qualities of silanamine. 63 That second complaint is therefore based on a misreading of the judgment under appeal and must accordingly be (2) Findings of the Court 200 It follows from paragraphs 233 to 242 of the judgment under appeal that the General Court examined whether the appellant had a right to be consulted on the RAC Opinion under Article 37(4) of the CLP Regulation and, as is apparent from paragraph 192 of the present judgment, correctly held that it did not have such a right. 201 Thus, even assuming that the General Court distorted the second part of the second plea raised at first instance by finding that that part referred to the right to be heard before the RAC concerning the opinion adopted by the RAC, that distortion would have had no effect on the examination of that part. 202 Therefore, the first subsection of the second part of the second ground of appeal must be rejected as ineffective. 203 Accordingly, the second part of the second ground of appeal must be rejected in its entirety. 204 In the light of all the foregoing, the second ground of appeal must be rejected in its entirety and, accordingly, the appeal must be dismissed. VI. Costs 205 In accordance with the Article 184(2) of the Rules of Procedure, where the appeal is unfounded, the Court is to make a decision as to costs. 206 Under Article 138(1) of the Rules of Procedure, which applies to appeal proceedings by virtue of Article 184(1) thereof, the unsuccessful party is to be ordered to pay the costs if they have been applied for in the successful party’s pleadings. 207 In the present case, since the Commission and ECHA have applied for costs against the appellant and the latter has been unsuccessful in its appeal, the appellant must be ordered to bear its own costs and to pay those incurred by the Commission and ECHA, in accordance with the forms of order sought by them. On those grounds, the Court (Third Chamber) hereby: 1. Dismisses the appeal; 2. Orders Evonik Operations GmbH to bear its own costs and to pay those incurred by the European Commission and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA).
