against a ground included for the sake of completeness – Plea in law ineffective (Art. 263 TFEU) (see paragraph 182, 183) Résumé The General Court upholds the decision of the European Commission refusing a request for an internal review of its Implementing Decision 2023/1319 ( 1 ) revising Member States’ annual greenhouse gas emission allocations for the period from 2023 to 2030. In so doing, it finds that such a request cannot call into question the legislative acts implemented by the decision in question. Regulation 2018/842 ( 2 ) set out Member States’ minimum contributions towards fulfilling the European Union’s target of reducing its greenhouse gas emissions by 30% below 2005 levels by 2030. Pursuant to that regulation, Implementing Decision 2020/2126 ( 3 ) set out the annual greenhouse gas emission allocations of the Member States for the period from 2021 to 2030. In accordance with the impact assessment accompanying a communication from the Commission of 17 September 2020 ( 4 ) (‘the impact assessment of 17 September 2020’), the European Climate Law ( 5 ) set a new net greenhouse gas emission reduction target of 55% compared to 1990 levels by 2030 (‘the 2030/1990 second target’). Regulation 2018/842 was then amended by Regulation 2023/857 ( 6 ) in order to include the European Union’s new target of reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 40% below 2005 levels by 2030 (‘the 2030/2005 second target’) and Implementing Decision 2023/1319 set new annual greenhouse gas emission allocations for Member States for the period from 2023 to 2025. The applicants, Global Legal Action Network and Climate Action Network Europe, submitted to the Commission, pursuant to Article 10(1) of the Aarhus Regulation, ( 7 ) a request for an internal review of that decision on the grounds of procedural and substantive failures affecting that decision, the 2030/1990 second target and the 2030/2005 second target. The Commission refused the request for internal review as inadmissible and, in the alternative, as unfounded by decision of 14 December 2023 (‘the contested decision’). Findings of the Court As the applicants allege that the Commission erred in its interpretation of the grounds of their request for internal review, the Court begins by observing that it is required to conduct a full review of whether the contested decision demonstrates a correct understanding of those grounds. That consideration does not affect the wide measure of discretion conferred on the Commission when making complex assessments, which is subject to a review restricted to manifest errors of assessment. In the present case, the Court considers that the Commission did not distort the grounds of the request for review. As regards, next, the argument that the Commission infringed Article 10(1) of the Aarhus Regulation by rejecting as inadmissible the grounds of the request for internal review relating to the 2030/1990 second target and the 2030/2005 second target, the Court observes that, when adopting Implementing Decision 2023/1319, the Commission exercised implementing powers under Article 291(2) TFEU. Therefore, it could not set the allocations of the Member States on the basis of greenhouse gas emission reduction targets that were different from those established by Regulation 2023/857 and the European Climate Law, which constitute the legislative acts forming the basis of Implementing Decision 2023/1319. Furthermore, in accordance with that provision of the Aarhus Regulation, a request for internal review of an administrative act is to be submitted to the institution that was competent to adopt it and that has the power to amend it in the event that that act is unlawful. Since it is not possible, with such a request, to call into question the basic legislation that was adopted by other institutions and which the Commission is required to apply by virtue of its implementing power, the Commission cannot examine the merits of grounds relating to that basic legislation during the internal review of that administrative act without running counter to the principles of institutional balance, of sincere cooperation and of the conferral of powers, but also to the principle of legal certainty. That finding is all the more compelling since, in accordance with the combined provisions of Article 2(1)(c) and Article 10(1) of the Aarhus Regulation, activities of the institutions are excluded from the scope of that regulation when they exercise legislative powers. In particular, a legislative act cannot be the subject of a request for internal review, which may concern only an ‘administrative act’ that contains provisions that may contravene environmental law within the meaning of Article 2(1)(f) of that regulation. Similarly, legislative acts are also excluded from the scope of the Aarhus Convention, with which the Aarhus Regulation must be interpreted consistently. Thus, in the context of a request for internal review formally directed at Implementing Decision 2023/1319, which constitutes a non-legislative act, the fact that legislative provisions such as those laying down the two abovementioned targets are contrary to environmental standards cannot be relied on. It is true that the concept of ‘environmental law’ within the meaning of Article 2(1)(f) of the Aarhus Regulation must be interpreted broadly and covers any EU act which, irrespective of its legal basis and whether or not it is legislative in nature, contributes to the achievement of the objectives of that policy. However, that interpretation must remain consistent with the strict delimitation of the Commission’s implementing powers and the principles of conferral of powers, of institutional balance, of sincere cooperation and of legal certainty. Consequently, the author of a request for internal review of an implementing act is not entitled to rely on rules of environmental law that, if such a request were granted, would have the effect of derogating from the basic legislation of that implementing act, thereby altering that legislation by supplementing or amending it, including as to its non-essential elements. As regards the complaints by which the applicants criticise the Commission for having rejected as inadmissible the grounds of their request for internal review directed against the impact assessment of 17 September 2020, the Court holds, first, that no provision of the Aarhus Regulation, read in the light of the Aarhus Convention, precludes legal defects in a preparatory measure, such as that assessment, from being relied on in an action directed against the definitive act for which it represents a preparatory step. Secondly, the Court holds that the applicants could rely on illegalities vitiating Implementing Decision 2023/1319 in the light of provisions other than those of Regulation 2018/842, ( 8 ) without, however, leading the Commission to derogate from the provisions of the European Climate Law and Regulations 2018/842 and 2023/857. The contested decision is thus vitiated by errors of law in respect of Article 2(1)(f) and Article 10 of the Aarhus Regulation, in so far as it states that the applicants could not rely incidentally on the illegality of the impact assessment of 17 September 2020 on the grounds that that act was of a preparatory character, and that Regulation 2018/842 constituted ‘the environmental law’ for the assessment of the legality of Implementing Decision 2023/1319. However, those defects cannot lead to the annulment of the contested decision, as the Commission was correct to reject the applicants’ claims directed against the abovementioned impact assessment, on account of its substantive link with the European Climate Law and Regulation 2018/842. Since the assessment was submitted by the Commission with a view to incorporating the 2030/1990 second target and the 2030/2005 second target into the EU legal order, it corresponds to a legislative proposal and thus amounts to the Commission acting in a legislative capacity within the meaning of Article 2(1)(c) of the Aarhus Regulation. Lastly, the Court rejects as ineffective the applicants’ arguments challenging the contested decision in so far as it refused the request for review as unfounded, since they are directed against grounds included in that decision for the sake of completeness. The Court therefore dismisses the action in its entirety.
