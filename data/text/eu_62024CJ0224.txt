By its first question, the referring court asks, in essence, whether Article 3(12) of Directive 2012/18 must be interpreted as precluding a practice, possibly included in the waste management permit issued by the competent administrative authority, according to which, for the purpose of assessing the ‘presence of dangerous substances’ in an establishment, within the meaning of that provision, and of proving that the quantities thereof at no time reach the quantities indicated in Part 1 or Part 2 of Annex I to that directive, it is not permissible to rely solely on a management system implemented by the operator of that establishment ensuring the continuous monitoring of such substances actually present within it. 40 In that connection, Article 2(1) of Directive 2012/18 states that that directive applies to establishments as defined in Article 3 thereof. As is apparent from a combined reading of Article 3(1) to (3) and (10), the concept of ‘establishment’ refers to the entire location under the control of an operator where dangerous substances covered by Part 1 or Part 2 of Annex I to that directive are present in one or more installations in quantities equal to or in excess of the quantities indicated in Annex I. 41 It follows that the quantities of dangerous substances present in an establishment determine whether that establishment falls within the scope of application of Directive 2012/18. In that way, establishments in which those substances are present in quantities below the thresholds indicated in Column 2 of Part 1 or Part 2 of Annex I to that directive do not fall within its scope. Furthermore, depending on those quantities, establishments are classified as ‘lower-tier establishments’ or ‘upper-tier establishments’ within the meaning of paragraphs 2 and 3 respectively of Article 3 of that directive. That classification determines the extent of the obligations imposed, under that directive, on the Member State and operator concerned. 42 Article 3(12) of Directive 2012/18, the interpretation of which is sought by the referring court, defines the concept of ‘presence of dangerous substances’ as the actual or anticipated presence of dangerous substances in the establishment, or of dangerous substances which it is reasonable to foresee may be generated during loss of control of the processes in any installation within the establishment, in quantities equal to or in excess of the ‘qualifying quantities’ set out in Part 1 or Part 2 of Annex I. 43 That provision thereby provides for three scenarios in which dangerous substances should be considered to be ‘present’ within an establishment, namely where such substances are found to actually be present in certain quantities, where that presence is anticipated or where it is reasonably foreseeable during loss of control of the processes. 44 Since the terms ‘actual presence’, ‘anticipated presence’ and ‘dangerous substances which it is reasonable to foresee may be generated during loss of control of the processes’ are not defined by Directive 2012/18 and since that directive does not refer to the law of the Member States to do so, it is necessary, in accordance with settled case-law, to determine the meaning and scope of those terms by reference to their usual meaning in everyday language, while account is also taken of the context in which they occur and the purposes of the rules of which they form part (see, to that effect, judgments of 18 October 2011, Brüstle , C‑34/10, EU:C:2011:669, paragraphs 25 and 31 and the case-law cited, and of 12 February 2026, Stichting Koskea , C‑490/24, EU:C:2026:89, paragraph 24). 45 In that connection, it should be noted, as regards, first of all, the usual meaning of those terms, first, that the ‘actual presence’ of a dangerous substance implies its actual or proven existence, secondly, that the ‘anticipated presence’ of such a substance can be understood to refer to a substance that it is foreseen or planned will be acquired or generated and, thirdly, that the presence of a dangerous substance which it is ‘reasonable to foresee’ may be generated during loss of control of the processes refers to the occurrence of an event which can reasonably be envisaged in given circumstances. 46 It follows that Article 3(12) of Directive 2012/18 refers to both the dangerous substances actually present, in certain quantities, within an establishment and to those whose presence within that establishment is foreseen or reasonably foreseeable in certain circumstances. Accordingly, the actual absence of such substances within an establishment at a given time is not sufficient in itself to exclude that establishment from the scope of application of Directive 2012/18 under Articles 2 and 3 thereof, since account should also be taken of their anticipated or reasonably foreseeable presence. 47 Next, it should be noted that that literal interpretation of Article 3(12) of Directive 2012/18 is supported by the context of that provision. 48 In particular, for the purpose of determining the quantities of dangerous substances present within an establishment, that provision refers to the ‘qualifying quantities’ set in Annex I to Directive 2012/18. Costs 76 Since these proceedings are, for the parties to the main proceedings, a step in the action pending before the referring court, the decision on costs is a matter for that court. Costs incurred in submitting observations to the Court, other than the costs of those parties, are not recoverable. On those grounds, the Court (Fourth Chamber) hereby rules: Article 3(12) of Directive 2012/18/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 4 July 2012 on the control of major-accident hazards involving dangerous substances, amending and subsequently repealing Council Directive 96/82/EC must be interpreted as not precluding a practice, possibly included in the waste management permit issued by the competent administrative authority, according to which, for the purpose of assessing the ‘presence of dangerous substances’ in an establishment, within the meaning of that provision, and of proving that the quantities thereof at no time reach the quantities indicated in Part 1 or Part 2 of Annex I to Directive 2012/18, it is not permissible to rely solely on a management system implemented by the operator of that establishment ensuring the continuous monitoring of such substances actually present within it.
