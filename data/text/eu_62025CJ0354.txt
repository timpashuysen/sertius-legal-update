Spina, acting as Agents, after hearing the Opinion of the Advocate General at the sitting on 11 June 2026, gives the following Judgment 1 This request for a preliminary ruling concerns the interpretation of Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) and (3) of Directive 2001/83/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 6 November 2001 on the Community code relating to medicinal products for human use (OJ 2001 L 311, p. 67, and corrigenda OJ 2009 L 87, p. 174, and OJ 2011 L 276, p. 63), as amended by Directive 2012/26/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 25 October 2012 (OJ 2012 L 299, p. 1) (‘Directive 2001/83’), and of Articles 34 and 36 TFEU. 2 The request has been made in proceedings between R. SpA, a company incorporated under Italian law which holds a marketing authorisation (‘MA’) granted in Germany in respect of a medicinal product for human use (‘the reference medicinal product’), and the Bundesrepublik Deutschland (Federal Republic of Germany) concerning the decision of the Bundesinstitut für Arzneimittel und Medizinprodukte (Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices, Germany; ‘the Federal Institute for Drugs’) to renew, for the benefit of A. AG, a company incorporated under German law, the parallel import licence for a medicinal product with similar effects, issued on the basis of the MA for the reference medicinal product. Legal context Directive 2001/83 3 Recitals 2 to 5, 14, 40 and 41 of Directive 2001/83 state: ‘(2) The essential aim of any rules governing the production, distribution and use of medicinal products must be to safeguard public health. (3) However, this objective must be attained by means which will not hinder the development of the pharmaceutical industry or trade in medicinal products within the [European Union]. (4) Trade in medicinal products within the [European Union] is hindered by disparities between certain national provisions, in particular between provisions relating to medicinal products … and such disparities directly affect the functioning of the internal market. (5) Such hindrances must accordingly be removed; whereas this entails approximation of the relevant provisions. … (14) This directive represents an important step towards achievement of the objective of the free movement of medicinal products. … … (40) The provisions governing the information supplied to users should provide a high degree of consumer protection, in order that medicinal products may be used correctly on the basis of full and comprehensible information. (41) The marketing of medicinal products whose labelling and package leaflets comply with this Directive should not be prohibited or impeded on grounds connected with the labelling or package leaflet.’ 4 Title I of that directive, entitled ‘Definitions’, includes Article 1 thereof, which provides: ‘For the purposes of this Directive, the following terms shall bear the following meanings: … 23. Immediate packaging: The container or other form of packaging immediately in contact with the medicinal product. 24. Outer packaging: The packaging into which is placed the immediate packaging. 25. Labelling: Information on the immediate or outer packaging. …’ 5 Article 2(1) of that directive provides: ‘This Directive shall apply to medicinal products for human use intended to be placed on the market in Member States and either prepared industrially or manufactured by a method involving an industrial process.’ 6 Title V of that directive, entitled ‘Labelling and package leaflet’, includes, inter alia, Articles 54, 55 and 63 of that directive. 7 Article 54 of Directive 2001/83 provides: ‘The following particulars shall appear on the outer packaging of medicinal products or, where there is no outer packaging, on the immediate packaging: (a) the name of the medicinal product followed by its strength and pharmaceutical form, and, if appropriate, [who] it is intended for …; (b) a statement of the active substances expressed qualitatively and quantitatively …; (c) the pharmaceutical form and the contents by weight, by volume or by number of doses of the product; (d) a list of those excipients …; (e) the method of administration and, if necessary, the route of administration. …; (f) a special warning that the medicinal product must be stored out of the reach and sight of children; (g) a special warning, if this is necessary for the medicinal product; (h) the expiry date in clear terms (month/year); (i) special storage precautions, if any; (j) specific precautions relating to the disposal of unused medicinal products or waste derived from medicinal products, where appropriate, as well as reference to any appropriate collection system in place; (k) the name and address of the [MA] holder and, where applicable, the name of the representative appointed by the holder to represent him; (l) the number of the authorisation for placing the medicinal product on the market; (m) the manufacturer’s batch number; (n) in the case of non-prescription medicinal products, instructions for use; (o) for medicinal products other than radiopharmaceuticals referred to in Article 54a(1), safety features enabling wholesale distributors and persons authorised or entitled to supply medicinal products to the public to: – verify the authenticity of the medicinal product, and – identify individual packs, as well as a device allowing verification of whether the outer packaging has been tampered with.’ 8 Article 54a of that directive states in paragraph 1 thereof: ‘Medicinal products subject to prescription shall bear the safety features referred to in point (o) of Article 54, unless they have been listed in accordance with the procedure pursuant to point (b) of paragraph 2 of this Article. Medicinal products not subject to prescription shall not bear the safety features referred to in point (o) of Article 54, unless, by way of exception, they have been listed in accordance with the procedure pursuant to point (b) of paragraph 2 of this Article, after having been assessed to be at risk of falsification.’ 9 Article 55(3) of that directive lists the particulars which are to appear on small immediate packaging units on which the particulars laid down in Articles 54 and 62 of that directive cannot be displayed. 10 Article 58 of Directive 2001/83 provides: ‘The inclusion in the packaging of all medicinal products of a package leaflet shall be obligatory unless all the information required by Articles 59 and 62 is directly conveyed on the outer packaging or on the immediate packaging.’ 11 Under Article 60 of that directive: ‘Member States may not prohibit or impede the placing on the market of medicinal products within their territory on grounds connected with labelling or the package leaflet where these comply with the requirements of this Title.’ 12 Article 63(1) and (3) of that directive is worded as follows: ‘1. The particulars for labelling listed in Articles 54, 59 and 62 shall appear in an official language or official languages of the Member State where the medicinal product is placed on the market, as specified, for the purposes of this Directive, by that Member State. … 3. Where the medicinal product is not intended to be delivered directly to the patient, or where there are severe problems in respect of the availability of the medicinal product, the competent authorities may, subject to measures they consider necessary to safeguard human health, grant an exemption to the obligation that certain particulars should appear on the labelling and in the package leaflet. They may also grant a full or partial exemption to the obligation that the labelling and the package leaflet must be in an official language or official languages of the Member State where the medicinal product is placed on the market, as specified, for the purposes of this Directive, by that Member State.’ 13 Article 70(1) of that directive states: ‘When a marketing authorization is granted, the competent authorities shall specify the classification of the medicinal product into: – a medicinal product subject to medical prescription, – a medicinal product not subject to medical prescription. To this end, the criteria laid down in Article 71(1) shall apply.’ 14 Under Article 71(1) of Directive 2001/83: ‘Medicinal products shall be subject to medical prescription where they: – are likely to present a danger either directly or indirectly, even when used correctly, if utilized without medical supervision, or – are frequently and to a very wide extent used incorrectly, and as a result are likely to present a direct or indirect danger to human health, or – contain substances or preparations thereof, the activity and/or adverse reactions of which require further investigation, or – are normally prescribed by a doctor to be administered parenterally.’ 15 Article 76(3) of that directive provides: ‘Any distributor, not being the [MA] holder, who imports a medicinal product from another Member State shall notify the [MA] holder and the competent authority in the Member State to which the medicinal product will be imported of his intention to import that product. …’ German law 16 The movement of medicinal products in Germany is governed by the Gesetz über den Verkehr mit Arzneimitteln (Law on the marketing of medicinal products) of 24 August 1976 (BGBl. 1976 I, p. 2444), in the version published on 12 December 2005 (BGBl. 2005 I, p. 3394) (‘the AMG’). Paragraph 10(1) and the third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG are the provisions by which Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) of Directive 2001/83 were transposed into German law. 17 According to Paragraph 10(1) of the AMG, finished products may be placed on the market within the scope of the AMG only if certain mandatory particulars are affixed in a manner understandable by the public in the German language to containers and, where appropriate, to outer packaging, which includes the name of the medicinal product followed by an indication of its strength and pharmaceutical form and, if appropriate, whether it is intended for infants, children or adults, unless that information is already included in that name. Those particulars also include batch identification, with the abbreviation ‘L’ if the medicinal product is placed on the market in batches, or with the manufacturing date if the medicinal product cannot be placed on the market in batches, the content by weight, by volume or by number of units, the method of administration and the expiry date with the wording ‘use by’. 18 The third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG reduces the list of mandatory particulars inter alia in respect of containers whose nominal capacity does not exceed 10 millilitres (ml). 19 Under point 7 of the first sentence of Paragraph 25(2) of the AMG, the MA in respect of a medicinal product may be refused, inter alia, if the placing on the market of that medicinal product would infringe legal provisions. In addition, it follows from the first sentence of Paragraph 31(3) of the AMG that the renewal of an MA is also precluded in such a case. The dispute in the main proceedings and the questions referred for a preliminary ruling 20 R. is the holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product, indicated for the treatment of advanced prostate cancer and subject to medical prescription. 21 That medicinal product is supplied in the form of a solution to be injected, sold in two thermoformed packaging trays in a cardboard box, each of which is sealed with plastic film. The two trays each contain a syringe, one to be screwed into the other for the preparation of that solution. One of the syringes contains a solvent and the other an active substance in powder form. 22 In order to prepare that solution, the content of one of the syringes must be mixed with that of the other. The medicinal product is for single use only and according to the package leaflet, that solution must be prepared and used immediately once the packaging trays have been opened. 23 In 2009, company A. applied to the Federal Institute for Drugs for an MA for a medicinal product to be imported as a parallel import into Germany from Italy, referring to the reference medicinal product. That institute granted that MA in July 2010. 24 In 2012, company A. also announced its intention to import that medicinal product into Germany from Romania and Poland. In July 2014, that company applied to the Federal Institute for Drugs for the renewal of the MA of that medicinal product, referring, again, to the reference medicinal product. 25 By decision of 8 September 2014 (‘the contested decision’), the Federal Institute for Drugs renewed the parallel import licence for that medicinal product, while releasing A. from the obligation to affix certain particulars required by the third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG, as well as from the obligation that the labelling should appear in the German language for certain particulars which were also mandatory under that provision. In particular, that decision stated that, since the opening of the thermoplastic trays had an impact on the stability of the medicinal product, the parallel importer should not have to open them in order to label the syringes for the application of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG. 26 In order nevertheless to ensure the clear identification of that medicinal product and the two syringes by means of their labelling, the parallel importer, pursuant to that decision, had to ensure that, in principle, at least the name of the medicinal product and its dosage, the indication A or B on the syringe (making it possible to distinguish clearly the two syringes from each other), the batch identification and the expiry date of the medicinal product, are mentioned in letters of the Latin alphabet on the syringes used to inject the medicinal product imported as a parallel import. 27 As is apparent from the order for reference, the previous holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product (‘the initial holder’), namely R.’s predecessor in law, lodged an objection against the contested decision with the Federal Institute for Drugs. 28 Since that objection was rejected by decision of 22 May 2015, the original holder brought an action before the Verwaltungsgericht (Administrative Court, Germany), alleging failure to comply with the labelling requirements laid down in the third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG. 29 That action was dismissed by the Verwaltungsgericht (Administrative Court) as inadmissible, on the ground that that holder did not have standing to bring proceedings, since it had not suffered any infringement of its rights as a result of the alleged infringement of the German provisions on the labelling of medicinal products. During the appeal proceedings before the Oberverwaltungsgericht (Higher Administrative Court, Germany), R. announced that it had acquired the MA for the reference medicinal product from the original holder and that it would continue the proceedings. 30 By judgment of 14 December 2021, the Oberverwaltungsgericht (Higher Administrative Court) held that R’s action was admissible but unfounded. That court held that it was not necessary to answer the question whether the contested decision was unlawful on account of the failure to comply with the labelling requirements set out in Paragraph 10(1) and the third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG and in Article 55(3) of Directive 2001/83, since that did not infringe R.’s rights on the ground that those provisions were not intended to protect the holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product in the Member State of importation, but solely to inform and protect patients. 31 R. brought an appeal on a point of law against that judgment before the Bundesverwaltungsgericht (Federal Administrative Court, Germany), which is the referring court, by which it claims, inter alia, that the contested decision is unlawful, since the MA granted to A. was granted in breach of the labelling requirements set out in the third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG, which infringes its rights. 32 The Bundesrepublik Deutschland (Federal Republic of Germany), supported by A., contends that, in the interests of the free movement of goods, the Federal Institute for Drugs was fully entitled to allow A., as a parallel importer, not to affix particulars in the German language to the immediate packaging. Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG authorises, in exceptional cases, the adoption of measures diverging from the requirements which it lays down. 33 The Bundesverwaltungsgericht (Federal Administrative Court) states that the alleged infringement of the national labelling requirements laid down in Paragraph 10(1), and the third sentence of Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG render the renewal of the MA for a medicinal product imported as a parallel import unlawful only if those requirements applied to such a medicinal product. 34 Since those national provisions transpose Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) of Directive 2001/83, the referring court seeks, first, to ascertain whether those provisions are applicable to the MA of a medicinal product imported as a parallel import and the renewal of such an MA. In that regard, it asks the Court about the material scope of that directive and, in particular, whether, by analogy with veterinary medicinal products imported as parallel imports, to which the provisions of Directive 2001/82/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 6 November 2001 on the Community code relating to veterinary medicinal products (OJ 2001 L 311, p. 1), relating inter alia to labelling, must be applied, as the Court held in paragraph 56 of the judgment of 27 October 2016, Audace and Others (C‑114/15, EU:C:2016:813), it must be held that medicinal products for human use which are the subject of parallel imports fall within the scope of those provisions of Directive 2001/83. 35 If that question is answered in the affirmative, in order for it to be able to ascertain whether the competent authority, in the present case the Federal Institute for Drugs, was justified in exempting the parallel importer from compliance with the labelling requirements laid down by the AMG, the referring court asks, secondly, about the scope of Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83. 36 In the event that the medicinal product at issue falls within the scope of Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83, the referring court finds, thirdly, that there is no legal basis, in German law, for exempting a parallel importer from its labelling obligations under Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG. That court is uncertain, therefore, as to the direct effect of Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83, which has not been transposed into national law or has been transposed only in part. In that regard, the referring court asks whether that provision appears, from the point of view of its content, to be unconditional or whether it merely offers the Member States the possibility, left to their discretion, of providing for a derogation in their national laws. 37 In the event that Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83 is to be interpreted as meaning that a medicinal product for human use subject to a prescription does not fall within the scope of that provision or that that provision does not have direct effect, the referring court asks the Court whether requiring a parallel importer of such a medicinal product to label its immediate packaging in the language of the Member State of importation is compatible with the rules on the free movement of goods, namely Articles 34 and 36 TFEU, even though such labelling has an impact on the stability of that medicinal product. 38 In those circumstances, the Bundesverwaltungsgericht (Federal Administrative Court) decided to stay the proceedings and to refer the following questions to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling: ‘(1) Do the labelling requirements laid down in [Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article] 63(1) of Directive [2001/83] apply to a medicinal product for which [an MA] has been issued in a Member State and the importation of which into another Member State constitutes a parallel import in relation to a medicinal product already covered by [an MA] in that second Member State? (2) Must Article 63(3) of Directive [2001/83] be interpreted as meaning that a medicinal product is not intended to be delivered directly to the patient if it is classified as a medicinal product subject to medical prescription? (3) Does Article 63(3) of Directive [2001/83] have direct effect, meaning that an operator wishing to import into Germany a medicinal product from another Member State may rely on that provision before the German courts against the defendant Federal Republic of Germany which has not transposed that provision into national law or has not transposed it fully? (4) Do Articles 34 and 36 TFEU preclude provisions of national and of EU law, which require the labelling of immediate packaging with certain minimum particulars in the language of the Member State of importation, being applied to a medicinal product imported [as a parallel import], if it is not possible to relabel the immediate packaging of the medicinal product imported [as a parallel import] in order to comply with those labelling requirements because of a significant impairment to its [stability] which would result from this?’ Consideration of the questions referred Admissibility 39 Without formally pleading that the request for a preliminary ruling is inadmissible, the Bundesrepublik Deutschland (Federal Republic of Germany) expressed, in its written observations, its doubts as to the admissibility of the four questions referred for a preliminary ruling on the ground that there was no infringement of an individual right of the holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product, since no protective effect arises from the labelling requirements imposed by Directive 2001/83 and by Paragraph 10(8) of the AMG. 40 In that regard, that party refers to the findings of the referring court according to which, under a national procedural provision, namely the first sentence of Paragraph 113(1) of the Verwaltungsgerichtsordnung (Law on procedure before the administrative courts, Germany), the action for annulment brought by the holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product, which that court considers to be admissible from the outset, would be held to be well founded if the renewal of the MA granted to the parallel importer were established to be unlawful. 41 That party submits that the Verwaltungsgericht (Administrative Court) dismissed the action brought by the holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product as inadmissible on the ground of lack of standing to bring proceedings, even though the referring court did not provide any guidance in that regard. If the labelling provisions did not confer a protective effect on the holder of the MA for the reference medicinal product, the four questions referred would be hypothetical, in so far as, in such a case, the answer to the question whether the decision to renew the MA for the medicinal product imported as a parallel import is lawful would become redundant. 42 It must be recalled, in that regard, that, according to settled case-law, a reference for a preliminary ruling is an instrument of cooperation between the Court of Justice and national courts, by means of which the Court provides national courts with the points of interpretation of EU law which they need in order to decide the disputes before them. In the context of that cooperation, it is solely for the national court, before which the dispute has been brought and which must assume responsibility for the subsequent judicial decision, to determine in the light of the particular circumstances of the case in the main proceedings both the need for a preliminary ruling in order to enable it to deliver judgment and the relevance of the questions which it submits to the Court. Where the questions submitted concern the interpretation or validity of a rule of EU law, the Court is in principle bound to give a ruling. It follows that a question referred for a preliminary ruling concerning EU law enjoys a presumption of relevance (see judgments of 29 November 1978, Redmond , 83/78, EU:C:1978:214, paragraph 25, and of 19 November 2020, ZW , C‑454/19, EU:C:2020:947, paragraphs 20 and 21 the case-law cited). The Court may refuse to rule on such a question only where it is quite obvious that the interpretation of EU law that is sought is unrelated to the actual facts of the main action or its object, where the problem is hypothetical, or where the Court does not have before it the factual or legal material necessary to give a useful answer to the questions submitted to it (see judgments of 15 December 1995, Bosman , C‑415/93, EU:C:1995:463, paragraph 61, and of 19 November 2020, ZW , C‑454/19, EU:C:2020:947, paragraph 22 and the case-law cited). 43 In the present case, it is clear that the defendant in the main proceedings disputes the referring court’s interpretation of a procedural provision of its own legal order, namely Paragraph 113(1) of the Verwaltungsgerichtsordnung (Law on procedure before the administrative courts), in so far as that court considered it appropriate to examine, first of all, the substance of the action before it before ruling on the admissibility of that action. 44 As regards complaints relating to the infringement of national procedural rules, the Court has already held that those complaints fall within the assessment of the referring courts and that it is not for the Court either to call that assessment into question or to determine whether the order for reference was made in accordance with the rules of national law governing the The first question 47 By its first question, the referring court asks, in essence, whether Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) and (3) of Directive 2001/83 must be interpreted as meaning that the labelling requirements laid down by those provisions apply to medicinal products for human use which are the subject of a parallel import. 48 In accordance with settled case-law, in interpreting a provision of EU law, it is necessary to consider not only its wording, but also the context in which it occurs and the objectives pursued by the rules of which it is part (see judgments of 17 November 1983, Merck , 292/82, EU:C:1983:335, paragraph 12, and of 4 September 2025, Kwizda Pharma II , C‑451/24, EU:C:2025:663, paragraph 55 and the case-law cited). 49 As regards, in the first place, the wording of Article 54 of Directive 2001/83, that article lists the particulars which must appear on the outer packaging or, where there is no outer packaging, on the immediate packaging of medicinal products. It is a list of particulars, such as, inter alia, the name of the medicinal product followed by its strength and pharmaceutical form, the composition of the medicinal product, a list of the excipients contained therein, the method of administration of that medicinal product and, if necessary, the route of administration, a special warning, the expiry date of the medicinal product, the special precautions to be taken, the name and address of the MA holder, the number of the MA, the manufacturer’s batch number, or, in addition, the safety features. 50 Article 55(3) of that directive specifies the particulars which must, at least, be present on the small immediate packaging units of medicinal products, on which the information provided for, inter alia, in Article 54 of that directive cannot be displayed. 51 As for Article 63 of Directive 2001/83, it lays down, in paragraph 1, the rule that the particulars for labelling listed, in particular, in Article 54 of that directive shall appear in an official language or official languages of the Member State where the medicinal product is placed on the market, as specified, for the purposes of that directive, by that Member State. 52 Under the first sentence of Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83, where the medicinal product is not intended to be delivered directly to the patient, or where there are severe problems in respect of the availability of the medicinal product, the competent authorities may, subject to measures they consider necessary to safeguard human health, grant an exemption to the obligation that certain particulars should appear on the labelling and in the package leaflet. Under the second sentence of Article 63(3), those authorities may also grant a full or partial exemption to the obligation that the labelling and the package leaflet must be in an official language or official languages of the Member State where the medicinal product is placed on the market, as specified, for the purposes of that directive, by that Member State. 53 In that regard, it should be recalled that a situation such as that at issue in the main proceedings, where a medicinal product covered by an MA in one Member State is imported into another Member State in which an essentially similar medicinal product is already the subject of an MA, constitutes a parallel import of a medicinal product (judgment of 25 November 2021, Delfarma , C‑488/20, EU:C:2021:956, paragraph 29). 54 Although the wording of Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) and (3) of Directive 2001/83 does not expressly refer to medicinal products for human use imported as parallel imports, it in no way follows that such medicinal products must be excluded from the respective scope of those provisions. 55 As regards, in the second place, the context of Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) and (3) of Directive 2001/83, it should be noted, first of all, that that directive establishes a Community code relating to medicinal products for human use, Title V of which, entitled ‘Labelling and package leaflet’, which includes those provisions, lays down rules relating in particular to the manner in which the labelling of such medicinal products must be carried out. 56 It must be observed that that Title V does not draw a distinction on the basis of the origin of the medicinal product, since the provisions which it contains refer in an abstract manner, for example, to the ‘placing on the market of medicinal products’, or to a ‘medicinal product’, as follows from Articles 60 and 61 of that directive, and, more explicitly, to ‘all medicinal products’, as is apparent from Article 58 of that directive. It follows that that Title V does not limit the scope of the rules on labelling contained therein solely to medicinal products which have obtained an MA under the national ordinary law procedure. 57 Next, it would be inconsistent from the point of view of the entire system of information for patients on medicinal products laid down by Directive 2001/83 to take the view that the EU legislature could have intended to apply the resulting rules, in particular as regards labelling, to certain medicinal products and not to others, in the present case those which are imported as parallel imports, even though they are identical or similar medicinal products. 58 Lastly, it is true that the Court has already held that Directive 2001/83 cannot apply to a medicinal product covered by an MA in one Member State which is being imported into another Member State as a parallel import of a medicinal product already covered by an MA in that other Member State, since that imported medicinal product cannot, in such a case, be regarded as being placed on the market for the first time in the Member State of importation, since such a situation falls under the provisions of the FEU Treaty on the free movement of goods (judgments of 16 December 1999, Rhône-Poulenc Rorer and May & Baker , C‑94/98, EU:C:1999:614, paragraph 27 and the case-law cited; of 3 July 2019, Delfarma , C‑387/18, EU:C:2019:556, paragraph 19 and the case-law cited; and of 8 October 2020, kohlpharma , C‑602/19, EU:C:2020:804, paragraph 25 and the case-law cited). 59 However, that interpretation cannot result in medicinal products for human use imported as parallel imports being removed from the labelling requirements laid down in Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) and (3) of Directive 2001/83, which are intended to ensure the protection of human health. 60 First, as the Advocate General observed in point 58 of his Opinion, by stating that ‘Directive 2001/83 cannot apply to’, the Court did not intend to exclude medicinal products imported as parallel imports from the scope of all the provisions of that directive. On the contrary, that case-law must be understood as meaning that it is only the provisions of that directive relating to the grant of an MA that cannot apply to such medicinal products. 61 Moreover, it should be observed that, in the analogous context of parallel imports of proprietary medicinal products, in the context of Council Directive 65/65/EEC of 26 January 1965 on the approximation of provisions laid down by law, regulation or administrative action relating to proprietary medicinal products (OJ, English Special Edition: Series I Volume 1965-1966, p. 20), or parallel imports of plant protection products, in the context of Council Directive 91/414/EEC of 15 July 1991 concerning the placing of plant protection products on the market (OJ 1991 L 230, p. 1), the Court has stated that it is indeed the requirements relating to the grant of an MA that should not be applied and that it was not necessary, in order to protect health, to subject parallel importers to the MA procedure, given that the competent authorities in the importing Member State already have all the information necessary for the exercise of that scrutiny (see, as regards Directive 65/65, judgment of 12 November 1996, Smith & Nephew and Primecrown , C‑201/94, EU:C:1996:432, paragraph 21, and, as regards Directive 91/414, judgment of 6 November 2014, Mac , C‑108/13, EU:C:2014:2346, paragraph 27). 62 Second, as the referring court has stated, the Court has adopted the same approach in the cont

The fourth question 100 By its fourth question, the referring court asks, in essence, whether Articles 34 and 36 TFEU must be interpreted as precluding the application of legislation of a Member State which requires, absolutely, that the immediate packaging of medicinal products for human use imported as parallel imports be labelled in the official language or languages of the Member State of importation, even though the relabelling of that packaging would adversely affect the stability of that medicinal product. 101 In view of the answer given to the third question, there is no need to examine the fourth question. Costs 102 Since these proceedings are, for the parties to the main proceedings, a step in the action pending before the referring court, the decision on costs is a matter for that court. Costs incurred in submitting observations to the Court, other than the costs of those parties, are not recoverable. On those grounds, the Court (Third Chamber) hereby rules: 1. Article 54, Article 55(3) and Article 63(1) and (3) of Directive 2001/83/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 6 November 2001 on the Community code relating to medicinal products for human use, as amended by Directive 2012/26/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 25 October 2012, must be interpreted as meaning that the labelling requirements which those provisions lay down apply to medicinal products for human use which are the subject of parallel imports. 2. The first sentence of Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83, as amended by Directive 2012/26, must be interpreted as meaning that a medicinal product is to be regarded as not being ‘intended to be delivered directly to the patient’, within the meaning of that provision, where it cannot be safely taken by a patient acting alone, since the administration of that medicinal product requires specific handling or supervision by a healthcare professional or healthcare establishment, irrespective of whether that medicinal product is subject to medical prescription. 3. Article 63(3) of Directive 2001/83, as amended by Directive 2012/26, must be interpreted as having direct effect in so far as it empowers the competent national authorities to decide on applications for exemption from the labelling requirements laid down by that directive.
