The dispute in the main proceedings and the question referred for a preliminary ruling 19 Payback is a company which manages the multi-partner bonus points programme, called ‘PAYBACK’. That programme makes it possible to retain and attract customers from the trading partners participating in it. When users of the scheme make purchases from a participating commercial partner, the latter may grant a discount on those purchases in the form of ‘PAYBACK points’, which can be used to make payments to those trading partners once a certain points threshold has been reached. 20 The Verband Wirtschaft im Wettbewerb is an association to protect competition. Under the Law against unfair competition, it is empowered to act in order to stop unfair competition practices. 21 On 1 February 2022, Payback promoted, in its online information letter, a prize draw, including the statement: ‘a new prize to be won every day, such as 2 x Samsung QLED 4K TV Q80A, 75-inch’. That statement was accompanied by an image of the television concerned and by a table setting out the prizes to be won. 22 It is apparent from the request for a preliminary ruling that neither the energy efficiency class of the television at issue in the main proceedings nor the range of energy efficiency classes for the corresponding product group were indicated in that online information letter, on the image of the television or in that table setting out the prizes to be won. 23 After the Verband Wirtschaft im Wettbewerb had unsuccessfully sent Payback a letter of formal notice asking it, in essence, to put an end to such practices, that association brought an action before the Landgericht München I (Regional Court, Munich I, Germany). 24 That court delivered a judgment in absentia, by which Payback was ordered, inter alia, to cease all visual advertisements for a specific model of electronic display without indicating the energy efficiency class or the corresponding range of energy efficiency classes on the energy label of that device such as are legally represented graphically in the form of an arrow. 25 Following an objection by Payback, that court upheld its default judgment. 26 Payback brought an appeal against that judgment before the Oberlandesgericht München (Higher Regional Court, Munich, Germany), which set aside that judgment and dismissed the action of the Verband Wirtschaft im Wettbewerb as being unfounded. 27 That court found that the information requirements at issue relate to commercial communications within the meaning of the Law against unfair competition. According to that court, Payback is not a ‘dealer’ within the meaning of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, and was therefore not required to comply with the obligations arising out of point (a) of the first subparagraph of Article 6 of that regulation, read in conjunction with Article 4(d) of Delegated Regulation 2019/2013. In that regard, that court took the view that a prize draw was not used to offer or display the goods at stake. Besides, the concept of ‘whether or not in return for payment’ referred to in the definition of ‘dealer’ is not intended to extend the class of dealers beyond persons who offer for sale, hire, or hire purchase, or display products to customers or installers. Lastly, it took the view that, by participating in the prize draw, customers did not choose a product, but solely decided to take part in the prize draw. 28 The Verband Wirtschaft im Wettbewerb brought an appeal on a point of law ( Revision ) before the Bundesgerichtshof (Federal Court of Justice, Germany), which is the referring court, against the judgment of the Oberlandesgericht München (Higher Regional Court, Munich), seeking to have that judgment set aside and to have the judgment of the Landgericht München I (Regional Court, Munich I) upheld. The Verband Wirtschaft im Wettbewerb submits, in essence, that the concepts of ‘sale’, ‘hire’ and ‘hire-purchase’ in Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369 should be interpreted broadly. Thus, any economic activity is sufficient for a retailer or another natural or legal person to be regarded as being a dealer within the meaning of that provision. 29 According to the Verband Wirtschaft im Wettbewerb, it follows from the clear wording of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369 that that provision also covers contracts entered into without consideration and sales entered into not in return for payment, namely gifts, such as the one at issue in the present case. The fact that in the German-language version of that provision, the term ‘ unentgeltlich ’ (corresponding to ‘not in return for payment’ in the English-language version) relates not only to displaying products but also to all forms of activity, and thus also to purchase, is made clear, inter alia, by the English-language version of that provision, in which the part of the wording that refers to a possible absence of consideration (‘whether or not in return for payment’) appears at the end of the definition of ‘dealer’, separated by a comma. 30 The referring court is of the view that, in order to resolve the dispute before it, it is necessary to determine whether the organiser of a prize draw who promises prizes to be won must be regarded as a ‘dealer’, within the meaning of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, and as such be subject to the obligations arising from point (a) of the first paragraph of Article 6 of that regulation, read in conjunction with Article 4(d) of Delegated Regulation 2019/2013. 31 In that regard, the referring court notes, in the first place, the concept of ‘ unentgeltlich … zum Kauf … anbietet ’ (‘sale … not in return for payment’) is not only unknown to German civil law but also to EU law. As regards the latter, the referring court points out that Directive (EU) 2019/771 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 20 May 2019 on certain aspects concerning contracts for the sale of goods, amending Regulation (EU) 2017/2394 and Directive 2009/22/EC, and repealing Directive 1999/44/EC (OJ 2019 L 136, p. 28) requires, in order for a contract to be regarded as a contract of sale, the payment of a price or an undertaking to pay the price. 32 In the second place, as regards the concept of ‘customer’, as defined in Article 2(16) of Regulation 2017/1369, the referring court acknowledges that the receipt of a product, within the meaning of that provision, does not involve any consideration. However, according to the referring court, it cannot be inferred from that absence of consideration that, as regards the concept of ‘sale’, within the meaning of Article 2(13) of that regulation, the EU legislature envisaged the possibility that no consideration would be paid. 33 In the third place, the referring court considers that the objectives pursued by Regulation 2017/1369, namely, first, to inform consumers about the energy efficiency of the appliances concerned during their use and about the energy consumption of energy-related products and, second, to enable consumers to make informed choices in favour of products that consume less energy, could support a broad interpretation of the concepts of ‘sale’, ‘hire’ and ‘hire-purchase’, within the meaning of that regulation. 34 In the fourth place, the referring court is of the view that the phrase ‘whether or not in return for payment’, contained in Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, could be interpreted as relating not to the sale, hire or hire-purchase referred to in that provision, but to the fact of ‘displaying’ or ‘offering’ a product. In such a case, it would be sufficient, in order for a person to act as a ‘dealer’, that he or she displays or offers a product, whether or not in return for payment, in order to carry out a sale, hire or hire-purchase. However, that is not the case here, since the television at issue in the main proceedings was not offered for sale, hire or hire-purchase. 35 In the fifth place, the referring court adds that it is conceivable that the circumstance of a person who ‘displays’ the product should be regarded as being distinct from the circumstance of a person who ‘offers’ that product for sale, hire or hire-purchase. If that is the case, those other elements of the definition of the concept of ‘dealer’, within the meaning of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, would not apply to a person who ‘displays’ a product, within the meaning of that provision. 36 In those circumstances, the Bundesgerichtshof (Federal Court of Justice) decided to stay the proceedings and to refer the following question to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling: ‘Is a person who offers a television as a prize in the context of an online prize draw to be regarded as a “dealer” within the meaning of Article 2(13) of Regulation [2017/1369], and therefore under an obligation to comply with the requirements arising from [point (a) of the first paragraph] of Article 6 of Regulation [2017/1369], read in conjunction with Article 4(d) of Delegated Regulation [2019/2013]?’ Consideration of the question referred 37 By its single question, the referring court asks, in essence, whether Article 2(13) of Regulation Literal Interpretation 40 According to the wording of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, the concept of ‘dealer’ means ‘a retailer or other natural or legal person who offers for sale, hire, or hire purchase, or displays products to customers or installers in the course of a commercial activity, whether or not in return for payment’. 41 In that regard, it should be noted that there is a difference in wording between the German-language version of that provision and several other language versions of it, such as, for example, the Greek-, English-, French-, Italian- and Slovenian-language versions of that provision. 42 Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, in the German-language version, is worded as follows: ‘“Händler” bezeichnet einen Einzelhändler oder eine andere natürliche oder juristische Person, die im Rahmen einer Geschäftstätigkeit entgeltlich oder unentgeltlich Produkte an bzw. für Kunden oder Errichter zum Kauf, zur Miete oder zum Ratenkauf anbietet oder ausstellt’ . Consequently, in that linguistic version of that provision, both the offer of a product and the activity of displaying it appear to be linked to the purpose of its sale, hire or hire-purchase. 43 By contrast, other linguistic versions of that provision, such as the Greek-, English-, Italian- and Slovenian-language versions, appear to distinguish between the offer of a product and its display. According to the Greek-language version: (‘[νοείται ως] “έμπορος”: ο λιανοπωλητής ή κάθε άλλο φυσικό ή νομικό πρόσωπο που προσφέρει προς πώληση, μίσθωση ή μίσθωση-αγορά ή εκθέτει προϊόντα σε πελάτες ή εγκαταστάτες στο πλαίσιο εμπορικής δραστηριότητας, είτε επί πληρωμή είτε δωρεάν’), the English-language version (‘“dealer” means a retailer or other natural or legal person who offers for sale, hire, or hire purchase, or displays products to customers or installers in the course of a commercial activity, whether or not in return for payment’), the Italian-language version ( ‘ [si intende per] “distributore”, il dettagliante o altra persona fisica o giuridica che offre in vendita, noleggio, oppure locazione-vendita o espone prodotti ai clienti o installatori nel corso di un’attività commerciale, a titolo oneroso o meno ’) and the Slovenian-language version (‘ “trgovec” pomeni prodajalca na drobno ali drugo fizično ali pravno osebo, ki izdelke strankam ponuja naprodaj, v najem ali v nakup s pridržanim lastništvom ali jih razstavlja, ali monterje, ki izvajajo gospodarsko dejavnost, bodisi odplačno ali neodplačno ’) of that provision, the ‘dealer’ is a person who offers for sale, hire, hire purchase or displays products in the course of a commercial activity, whether or not in return for payment. 44 The French-language version of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369 is worded in a manner comparable to those language versions, since the dealer is defined as ‘ un détaillant ou une autre personne physique ou morale qui vend, loue, offre en location-vente ou expose des produits à l’intention des clients ou des installateurs dans le cadre d’une activité commerciale, à titre onéreux ou gratuit’ , without, however, the term ‘ offre ’ being directly linked to the sale or hire of the product. 45 Accordingly, it is apparent from the wording of the German-language version of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369 that the ‘display’ of goods leads to the classification of a person as a ‘dealer’ if that display is carried out in the course of a commercial activity and for the purposes of the sale, hire or hire-purchase of that product, those two conditions being cumulative, whereas it follows from the wording of the other language versions of that provision, referred to in paragraphs 43 and 44 of the present judgment, that the display of a product in the course of a commercial activity is sufficient for the purposes of that classification. 46 In contrast, it should be noted that the clarification ‘whether or not in return for payment’ in Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369 refers, in all the language versions of that provision, to sale, hire, hire purchase as well as display. 47 Consequently, it cannot be ruled out, on reading the wording of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, that the concept of ‘dealer’ within the meaning of that provision may be interpreted as meaning that it covers persons who display an energy-related product in the course of a commercial activity for reasons other than obtaining financial consideration. 48 Nevertheless, it must be borne in mind that, according to settled case-law, in particular where there is divergence between the various language versions of a provision of EU law, the latter must be interpreted by reference to the purpose and general scheme of the rules of which it forms part (judgments of 27 October 1977, Bouchereau , 30/77, EU:C:1977:172, paragraph 14, and of 4 September 2025, Hakam p , C‑203/24, EU:C:2025:662, paragraph 34). 49 The legislative history of a provision of EU law may also reveal elements that are relevant to its interpretation (judgment of 8 May 2025, Provincie Oost-Vlaanderen and Sogent , C‑236/24, EU:C:2025:321, paragraph 21 and the case-law cited). The purpose and origin of Regulation 2017/1369 50 As regards the objectives pursued by Regulation 2017/1369, it is apparent from Article 1 thereof that that regulation provides for the labelling of energy-related products to enable customers to choose more efficient products in order to reduce their energy consumption. 51 In addition, it follows from recitals 2 and 10 thereof that the objectives of Regulation 2017/1369 are, inter alia, to provide accurate, relevant and comparable information on the energy consumption of energy-related products, in order to enable customers to make informed choices and to facilitate their choice of products that consume less energy, as well as to promote innovation and investment in the production of more energy-efficient products. 52 It should be noted, as the Advocate General observed in points 64 and 76 of her Opinion, that customers attach considerable value to information on the energy performance of a product when they decide to purchase or use it, with the result that they may be deterred from accepting less efficient energy-related products which are therefore likely to entail higher operating costs. Furthermore, energy labelling requirements aim to reward the best-performing products through a mandatory rating, by offering an advantage to businesses that offer more-energy-efficient products. 53 To interpret the concept of ‘dealer’ within the meaning of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369 restrictively, by excluding persons who display an energy-related product in the course of a commercial activity for reasons other than obtaining financial consideration, would risk jeopardising the attainment of those objectives. 54 It should also be noted that the objectives referred to in paragraph 51 of the present judgment fall within the framework of broader objectives. Recital 8 of Regulation 2017/1369 states, in that regard, that improving the efficiency of energy-related products through the informed choice of customers contributes to the achievement of the European Union’s energy efficiency objectives for the years 2020 and 2030 and the environmental and climate change objectives of the European Union. In addition, it is intended to have a positive effect on the environmental performance of energy-related products. 55 As the Advocate General pointed out, in essence, in point 83 of her Opinion, the attainment of those broader environmental protection objectives depends on the ability to deter customers from using products that are less energy-efficient and to encourage businesses to manufacture, as a matter of priority, more energy-efficient products. 56 It follows that, in order to ensure the full effectiveness of the objectives pursued by the energy labelling system established by Regulation 2017/1369, the concept of ‘dealer’ within the meaning of Article 2(13) of that regulation must be interpreted as encompassing persons who display an energy-related product in the course of a commercial activity for reasons other than obtaining financial consideration. 57 As regards the origin of Regulation 2017/1369, it must be borne in mind that, during the legislative process which led to the adoption of that regulation, significant amendments were made, in particular, as regards the definition of the concept of ‘dealer’. Commission proposal COM (2015) 341 final of 15 July 2015, which led to the adoption of Regulation 2017/1369, defined that concept as ‘a retailer or any other person who sells, leases, offers for hire purchase or displays products to consumers’, without referring to the words ‘whether or not in return for payment’. Thus, the definition of the concept of ‘dealer’ in that proposal was more restrictive than that adopted in Regulation 2017/1369. 58 It does not therefore appear that the EU legislature wished to limit the scope of the concept of ‘dealer’ in Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369, so as to exclude persons who display an energy-related product in the course of a commercial activity for reasons other than obtaining financial consideration. The general scheme of Regulation 2017/1369 59 In order to answer the question raised by the referring court, it is also necessary to determine whether a dealer who, in the course of his or her commercial activity, organises an online prize draw and promises an energy-related product as a prize to be won must be regarded as ‘displaying’ the product at issue to a ‘customer’ in the course of a ‘commercial activity’, whether or not in return for payment, within the meaning of Article 2(13) of Regulation 2017/1369. 60 It is necessary, in that regard, to examine the general scheme of that regulation and, more specifically, the concepts of ‘customer’, ‘display’, ‘commercial activity’ and ‘visual advertisements’ within the meaning of that regulation. 61 In order to provide the referring court with a useful answer, it is necessary, in the first place, to determine whether the concept of ‘customer’ within the meaning of Regulation 2017/1369 refers to a person who participates in a prize draw in the context of which the participants have the chance of winning, as a prize, an energy-related product. 62 In accordance with Article 2(16) of that regulation, a natural or legal person who buys, hires or receives a product comes within the concept of ‘customer’. 63 It should be stated that the EU legislature did not specify whether the expression ‘receives a product’ in that provision necessarily relates to a type of transfer of ownership or of making a product available for use for consideration other than sale, hire or hire purchase. 64 However, as the Advocate General stated in point 100 of her Opinion, given that the main contracts for consideration, namely sale and hire, are already set out in that provision, and in the absence of any specific clarification, it must be held that that expression ‘receives a product’ used in Article 2(16) of Regulation 2017/1369 must be interpreted as not being limited to the situation in which a customer receives a product from a dealer for consideration, but as also covering the situation in which a customer receives a product free of charge. 65 That interpretation is supported by the fact that recitals 2 and 10 and Article 1 of Regulation 2017/1369, which set out the objectives pursued by that regulation, refer to the concept of ‘customer’ by emphasising, without mentioning the concept of ‘purchase’, that the purpose of energy labelling is to provide accurate, relevant and comparable information on energy consumption of energy-related products so that ‘customers’ can make informed choices based on the energy consumption of those products. Similarly, by laying down detailed requirements relating to energy labelling for specific product groups, Regulation 2017/1369 also uses, in Article 16(3)(b) thereof, which concerns the design and content of the energy label, the concept of ‘customers’, without any reference to ‘purchase’ as such. 66 Such an interpretation is also supported by the fact that Regulation 2017/1369 replaced Directive 2010/30. The latter referred not to ‘customers’ but to ‘end-users’ making their ‘purchasing decisions’. That was the case, in particular, with recital 14, Article 2(g), which defined the concept of ‘dealer’, and Article 10, which provided that ‘provisions in delegated acts regarding information provided on the label and in the fiche … shall enable end-users to make better informed purchasing decisions’. That marked difference in the wording of Regulation 2017/1369 as compared to Directive 2010/30, which preceded it, reflects the intention of the EU legislature to extend the scope of the energy labelling requirements also to persons who are promised, free of charge, energy-related products. 67 In the light of the foregoing, it must be held that the concept of ‘customer’, within the meaning of Article 2(16) of Regulation 2017/1369, covers a person who participates in a prize draw in the context of which the participants have the chance to win, as a prize, an energy-related product. 68 It should be added, in that regard, that to consider, as Payback submits, that such a person ‘receives’ only a hope of winning a television and not the product itself, would, as the Advocate General stated, in essence, in point 68 of her Opinion, amount to a restrictive interpretation of that concept capable of undermining the attainment of the objectives pursued by Regulation 2017/1369. 69 It is necessary
